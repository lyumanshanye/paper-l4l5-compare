### A. Experimental details<br>
<br>
To measure the THz waveforms generated by our spintronic emitters, we employed a standard terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS) system, which has been described in detail in references [8, 10]. Think of THz-TDS as a very fast &quot;stopwatch&quot; for light: it fires an ultrashort laser pulse, then measures the electric field of the resulting terahertz wave at precise time delays, building up a complete picture of the waveform point by point. This system is driven by a femtosecond Ti:Sa laser—a workhorse of ultrafast optics—that delivers pulses shorter than 100 femtoseconds (that&#x27;s less than one millionth of one billionth of a second!) at a repetition rate of 75 MHz, meaning it fires 75 million pulses every second. The average output power is typically 600 mW, which is enough to excite the emitter efficiently. Alternatively, we could switch to a fiber laser operating at a wavelength of 1.55 µm (in the infrared region), with a repetition rate of 100 MHz and a lower output power of up to 50 mW. This flexibility allows us to test how the emitter responds to different pump wavelengths, which will become important later.<br>
<br>
The laser beam is first split into two parts using a 90:10 beam-splitter. The stronger portion (90%) is directed through a mechanical, computer-controlled delay line—essentially a movable mirror that changes the path length, and thus the arrival time, of the pump pulse. This delay line is crucial because it lets us sample the THz waveform at different time points. The weaker portion (10%) is used to &quot;gate&quot; the detector: it triggers a photoconductive switch, a tiny dipole antenna with a 20 µm gap, which acts as a fast electronic shutter to capture the THz field at a specific moment. By scanning the delay line, we map out the entire THz pulse shape.<br>
<br>
In this setup, the usual photoconductive antenna (PCA) emitter is replaced by our spintronic (ST) sample, which is a bilayer of iron (Fe) and platinum (Pt). The sample is mounted perpendicular to the direction of the pump beam, meaning the laser hits it head-on. A weak external magnetic field of 20 mT (millitesla—about 400 times weaker than a typical fridge magnet) is applied perpendicular to the pump beam. This field serves a critical purpose: it saturates the magnetization of the Fe layer along its easy axis (the direction in which it naturally prefers to magnetize). Why does this matter? Because the direction of the magnetic field dictates the polarization of the emitted THz field. Specifically, the THz field is always polarized perpendicular to both the pump beam direction and the magnetic field direction. Since the PCA detector is sensitive to the polarization of the incoming THz wave, we must ensure this polarization is stable and well-defined. That&#x27;s why we carefully control both the external magnetic field and the orientation of the Fe/Pt emitter&#x27;s easy axis—any drift would cause the detector to lose sensitivity.<br>
<br>
**Figure 1** illustrates the full THz time-domain emission spectroscopy (THz-TDES) setup used to measure the emission from our optimized Fe/Pt spintronic emitter. The optical pump beam is sharply focused using an aspherical short-focus lens down to a spot diameter of less than 10 µm in the plane of the metal layers. Why so tight? Because we want to excite a very small, well-defined region of the Fe layer. When the pump pulse hits the Fe, it excites spin-polarized electrons—electrons that have a preferred spin direction due to the magnetization. These hot electrons diffuse into the adjacent Pt layer, creating a spin-current. In the Pt layer, due to a phenomenon called the inverse spin Hall effect, this spin-current is converted into a transient transverse electric current. This current transient is the source of the THz pulse: it radiates an electromagnetic wave with a sub-picosecond duration (less than one trillionth of a second) and a center wavelength around 300 µm. That wavelength corresponds to a frequency of about 1 THz, which is in the terahertz gap—a region of the electromagnetic spectrum between microwaves and infrared.<br>
<br>
This THz pulse is emitted both forward and backward into free space, but it is not a nice, collimated beam. Instead, it emerges as a strongly divergent beam, spreading out in all directions. Why? Because the source is tiny (10 µm) compared to the wavelength (300 µm), the beam diffracts immediately—a fundamental property of waves. To capture this spreading radiation, we attach a hyperhemispherical silicon (Si) lens to the back side of the sample. This lens collects the divergent THz emission and converts it into a more manageable cone-shaped beam, which is then directed to an off-axis parabolic mirror for collimation (making the rays parallel). The Si-lens is a game-changer: with it attached, we observe an enhancement factor of up to 30 in the electric field amplitude. To understand why, imagine trying to catch rain with a thimble versus a funnel. Without the lens, most of the THz radiation would miss the first parabolic mirror and be lost, drastically reducing the efficiency of the entire optical system. The Si-lens acts as the funnel, capturing the wide-angle emission and funneling it into the detection path.<br>
<br>
Now, you might wonder: does the Si-lens itself affect the measurements? Yes, but here&#x27;s the key insight: since we use the same Si-lens for all comparative measurements, any wavelength-dependent effects are identical across experiments. So, when we compare results from different pump wavelengths, the only differences we see are due to the emitter&#x27;s intrinsic behavior, not the optics.<br>
<br>
After the first parabolic mirror collimates the THz beam, a second, identical off-axis parabolic mirror in a reversed configuration focuses the beam onto a second Si lens. This lens then concentrates the THz radiation through the GaAs substrate of the detector PCA and onto the dipole gap, where it is detected. In this way, the entire THz optical system—two Si-lenses and two parabolic mirrors—acts as a precise imaging system. It images the point source of THz waves on the emitter surface onto the gap of the detector, ensuring an efficient transfer of the emitted THz energy from source to detector. This is analogous to a projector lens focusing an image onto a screen: the source is the &quot;object,&quot; the detector gap is the &quot;image,&quot; and the optics ensure that the image is sharp and bright.<br>
<br>
In our setup, the THz beam path is fixed by the silicon lens on the emitter, the parabolic mirrors, and the silicon lens on the photoconductive antenna. Importantly, the alignment of these components is not changed when we exchange the spintronic emitter. If we also keep the position of the pump beam focus constant, we can easily swap emitters without realigning the beam path. This is because the lateral position of the focus on the emitter is not critical, as long as the layer structure is homogeneous. This design makes the experiment highly reproducible and efficient.<br>
<br>

With a 20 µm dipole, the detector response peaks around 1 THz, with a 50% reduction in sensitivity at 330 GHz and 2 THz. <td valign="top"><div>## II. EXPERIMENT  <br>
### A. Experimental details  <br>
<br>
To study the generation of terahertz (THz) radiation from spintronic materials, we use a technique called **terahertz time-domain spectroscopy**, or **THz-TDS**. This method allows us to not only generate short bursts of THz waves but also to measure their electric field directly over time—giving us both amplitude and phase information, which is incredibly powerful for understanding the physics of emission. The full setup is illustrated in Figure 1, and we’ll walk through it step by step, explaining not just *what* each part does, but *why* it’s designed that way.<br>
<br>
At the heart of the system is a **femtosecond laser**—a laser that emits extremely short pulses of light, each lasting less than 100 femtoseconds (that’s 0.0000000000001 seconds!). This laser serves as the &quot;engine&quot; driving the entire experiment. In our case, we have two possible lasers we can use:<br>
<br>
- A **Ti:sapphire (Ti:Sa) laser** that pulses at a rate of 75 million times per second (75 MHz), with an average power of about 600 milliwatts.<br>
- Alternatively, a **fiber laser** operating at a longer wavelength (1.55 micrometers), pulsing even faster (100 MHz), but with lower power (up to 50 mW).<br>
<br>
Why do we have two options? Because one of our scientific goals is to compare how different wavelengths of light affect the THz emission from our spintronic devices. But more on that later.<br>
<br>
Now, here’s a key idea: THz-TDS is a **pump-probe** technique. That means we split the laser beam into two parts:<br>
<br>
- The **pump beam** (the stronger one) is used to *excite* the sample and generate THz radiation.<br>
- The **probe beam** (the weaker one) is used to *detect* the THz wave at a precisely controlled time delay.<br>
<br>
The splitting is done using a **90:10 beam splitter**—90% of the light goes to the pump, 10% to the probe. The pump beam is sent through a **mechanical delay line**, which is essentially a movable mirror on a motorized stage. By changing the position of this mirror, we can control exactly when the pump pulse arrives at the sample relative to the probe pulse. This allows us to &quot;scan&quot; through the THz waveform in time, building it up point by point—like taking a slow-motion movie of an invisible electromagnetic wave.<br>
<br>
The pump beam hits a **spintronic (ST) emitter**, specifically a thin bilayer made of **iron (Fe)** and **platinum (Pt)**. This replaces the traditional photoconductive antenna (PCA) used in standard THz-TDS systems. So instead of using a semiconductor switch to generate THz waves, we’re using the quantum property of electron **spin**—hence the name *spintronic*. We’ll dive deeper into how this works in a moment.<br>
<br>
The sample is mounted so that the laser hits it **perpendicularly**—that is, head-on. But there’s another crucial ingredient: a **weak external magnetic field**, about 20 millitesla (mT), applied perpendicular to the laser beam. Why? Because the Fe layer is magnetic, and we need to align all the electron spins in a specific direction—this is called **saturating the magnetization** along the **easy axis** of the material. Think of it like combing tangled hair: without the magnetic field, the spins point in random directions; with it, they all line up neatly.<br>
<br>
This alignment is critical because the **polarization** (i.e., the direction of oscillation) of the emitted THz wave depends on the direction of the magnetization. Specifically, the THz electric field is always **perpendicular to both the pump laser beam and the magnetic field direction**. So by controlling the magnetic field, we control the polarization of the THz wave. This also means the detector must be aligned to match this polarization—otherwise, it won’t &quot;see&quot; the signal properly.<br>
<br>
On the detection side, we use a **photoconductive antenna (PCA)** with a tiny **20-micrometer dipole antenna**. The probe beam &quot;gates&quot; this antenna—meaning it briefly turns it on, allowing it to sample the instantaneous strength of the THz electric field passing through it. By repeating this measurement at many different delay times, we reconstruct the full THz waveform.<br>
<br>
Now let’s follow the THz wave on its journey from emitter to detector. When the pump laser hits the Fe/Pt bilayer, it excites **spin-polarized electrons** in the iron. These electrons carry their spin orientation as they move into the platinum layer, creating a **spin current**. But platinum has a strong **spin-orbit coupling**—a quantum effect that converts spin current into a **transverse charge current** (a regular electric current flowing perpendicular to the spin flow). This ultrafast current surge—lasting less than a picosecond—radiates a burst of THz light, emitted both forward and backward from the sample.<br>
<br>
Here’s a big challenge: THz waves have **long wavelengths**—around **300 micrometers** (which corresponds to a frequency of about 1 THz). But the source—the laser spot—is tiny, only **less than 10 micrometers in diameter**. When a wave is emitted from a source much smaller than its wavelength, it **spreads out rapidly in all directions**—a phenomenon called **diffraction**. Without any help, the THz beam would instantly fan out into a hemisphere, and most of it would miss the collection optics entirely.<br>
<br>
To solve this, we attach a **hyperhemispherical silicon (Si) lens** to the back of the sample. This lens acts like a funnel, capturing the widely spreading THz radiation and redirecting it into a more manageable, cone-shaped beam. This is a huge deal: experiments show this lens boosts the detected **electric field amplitude by up to 30 times**! Why such a dramatic improvement? Because it effectively reduces the angular spread of the emission right at the source, making the beam much easier to collect and guide.<br>
<br>
After the Si lens, the beam hits an **off-axis parabolic mirror**, which **collimates** it—meaning it makes the rays parallel, like turning a flashlight beam into a laser-like beam. A second, identical parabolic mirror (in reverse configuration) then **refocuses** the beam. Finally, another Si lens on the detector side focuses the THz radiation precisely onto the **20-µm gap** of the photoconductive antenna, where it can be measured.<br>
<br>
So the full optical path is:  <br>
**Emitter → Si lens → Parabolic mirror (collimator) → Parabolic mirror (focuser) → Si lens on detector → PCA gap**<br>
<br>
This entire system acts like an **imaging system**: it maps the point-like THz source on the emitter directly onto the tiny detection gap. This ensures **maximum transfer efficiency**—we’re not losing precious signal along the way.<br>
<br>
An important practical detail: once this optical path is aligned, we **don’t change it** when swapping out different spintronic emitters. As long as the new sample has the same Si lens attached and the pump laser is focused to the same spot, the THz beam path remains unchanged. And because the laser spot is small and the layers are uniform, the exact lateral position on the sample isn’t critical. This makes comparing different samples fast and reliable.<br>
<br>
Now, what about the **bandwidth**—how much of the THz spectrum can we actually detect? That’s limited by the **photoconductive dipole antenna** on the detector. The 20-µm gap size sets the response characteristics. Generally, smaller gaps allow detection of higher frequencies, but there’s a trade-off with signal strength.<br>
<br>
For our 20-µm dipole, the detector is most sensitive around **1 THz**. The response drops to half (−3 dB) at about **330 GHz** and **2 THz**. At the extremes, it falls to 10% of peak response at **100 GHz** and **3 THz**. This means our system can reliably capture signals from roughly **0.1 THz to 3 THz**, with the strongest sensitivity in the middle.<br>
