of the nested (blue line), nested5 (orange), nested10 (light blue),<br>
random lines (red) and random links (cyan) models. The colored<br>

<br>
Before we dive into the figure, let’s pause and make sure we understand what we’re looking at. The quantity being plotted, /C22QQT (d), is the *average asymmetry* of subgraphs of a given degree *d*. Think of it as a measure of how &quot;lopsided&quot; the network’s local structure is at different scales. A value near zero means the network is highly symmetric at that scale; a large value means the structure is heavily skewed. The fact that we’re averaging over 20 realizations (i.e., 20 independently generated versions of each model) tells us that we’re looking for *typical* behavior, not a one-off quirk. The colored area around each line is the standard error, which is a measure of how much we’d expect the average to vary if we repeated the experiment. A narrow band means the model is very consistent; a wide band means the results are more scattered.<br>
<br>
Now, why do we care about these five models? The nested5 and nested10 models are *intermediate* versions of the nested model. Think of the nested model as a perfectly ordered, hierarchical structure—like a well-organized library where every book has a precise, logical place. The random lines model is a more disordered version, where the long-range order is still present but the links are shuffled. The nested5 and nested10 models sit in between: they are created by taking the nested model and swapping a certain number of lines (5 and 10, respectively). As you swap more lines, you introduce more disorder and asymmetry. So, the progression from nested → nested5 → nested10 → random lines is a journey from perfect order to increasing chaos, while still retaining some long-range memory of the original structure.<br>
<br>
This is a crucial point: the nested, nested5, nested10, and random lines models all share a key feature—they have *long-range order* in the link strength. That means the strength of a connection between two nodes is not random; it depends on their positions in the hierarchy. In contrast, the random links model has *no* such correlation—each link’s strength is independent of the others. This fundamental difference in architecture is exactly what we see in the asymmetry values: the random lines and random links models behave very differently in Fig. 5(c), even though they might look similar at first glance. The asymmetry metric is sensitive enough to detect this qualitative difference in the underlying design.<br>
<br>
Now, let’s shift our focus to a different but related measure: the cumulative size distribution, P(Awa). This is a way of summarizing the sizes of the “areas” associated with the nodes of the nesting tree. If you imagine the network as a set of nested loops, each node in the tree corresponds to a loop, and the area A is a measure of the size of that loop. The cumulative distribution P(Awa) tells us the probability that a randomly chosen loop has an area greater than or equal to A. It’s a powerful tool because it reveals the *scale* of the structures in the network—whether there are many small loops, many large ones, or a mix.<br>
<br>
In Fig. 6(a), we plot P(Awa) for the peaks (green), gradient (magenta), nested (blue), and random links (cyan) models. Look at the gradient model: its curve is a straight line with a slope of 1/2 (the small deviation for small areas is just a boundary effect, not a real feature). This straight line is a signature of a *scale-invariant* structure—meaning the network looks the same at all scales, like a fractal. The peaks model, on the other hand, shows kinks and discontinuities in its slope. These kinks are not just noise; they are *informative*. They indicate that the architecture changes qualitatively at certain length scales. For example, a kink might signal the transition from a region of densely packed small loops to a region of sparser, larger loops.<br>
<br>
The nested and random lines models have curves that are significantly different from the gradient model. This tells us that their architecture is not scale-invariant in the same way. But how can we test for scale invariance more robustly? The answer is to define the *adjusted* cumulative size distribution, a:P(Awa). Here’s the logic: for self-similar graphs (like the nested model), we expect P(Awa) to be proportional to 1/a. If that’s true, then multiplying P(Awa) by a should give a constant value. So, if we plot a:P(Awa) and it fluctuates around a horizontal line, that’s strong evidence that the network is self-similar. The *nature* of the fluctuations—whether they are smooth, periodic, or erratic—tells us about the topology of the iterative building block of the nested model.<br>
<br>
In Fig. 6(b), we see a:P(Awa) for the random links model. The curve is not flat; it deviates significantly from a straight line. This confirms that the random links model is *not* self-similar. In Fig. 6(c), we see the same plot for the nested, nested5, nested10, and random lines models. As expected, all of these self-similar models produce curves that fluctuate around a straight line. But notice the *periodicity* in the nested model’s curve. This periodicity is a fingerprint of the architectural unit—the basic building block that is repeated at every scale. The period of the oscillation tells you the size of that unit. As we increase the disorder (moving from nested to nested5 to nested10 to random lines), the periodicity becomes less and less pronounced, until it disappears entirely in the random lines model. This makes sense: the more you scramble the structure, the less you see the repeating pattern.<br>
<br>
The deviation from a straight line for large a is due to boundary effects—the finite size of the network. When you’re looking at very large areas, you’re essentially probing the edges of the network, where the structure is inevitably truncated. This is a common limitation in any finite system, and it’s important to recognize it so you don’t misinterpret the data.<br>

<td valign="top"><div>the noise. In Fig. 5(c) we plot the mean $\langle \mathcal{C}_{22}^{QT}(d) \rangle$ over 20 realizations of the nested (blue line), nested5 (orange), nested10 (light blue), random lines (red), and random links (cyan) models. The colored area around each curve represents the standard error, which gives us a sense of how much variation there is across different realizations of the same model—tighter bands mean more consistent results.<br>
<br>
Let’s take a moment to understand what these different models represent. The **nested model** is our most ordered structure—it&#x27;s like a perfectly nested set of Russian dolls, where each level of the network mirrors the one above it in a self-similar way. This kind of organization leads to long-range correlations in how strong the connections (or &quot;link strengths&quot;) are across the network.<br>
<br>
Now, what about **nested5** and **nested10**? These are variations of the nested model where we’ve intentionally introduced some disorder to study how robust the nested structure’s properties are. Specifically, in nested5, we randomly swap the positions of 5% of the hierarchical levels (or &quot;lines&quot;) in the architecture; in nested10, we do this for 10%. Think of it like taking a neatly organized bookshelf and randomly swapping a few shelves—just enough to disrupt the perfect order, but not so much that all structure is lost. As we increase the number of swaps, the system becomes more disordered and asymmetric, creating a smooth transition between the perfectly nested world and the completely random one.<br>
<br>
Then we have two types of random models: **random lines** and **random links**. In **random lines**, the overall hierarchical layout (the &quot;lines&quot; or levels of organization) is shuffled, but the pattern of strong and weak connections still follows a broad structural template—so there&#x27;s still some long-range order in link strength. In contrast, the **random links** model completely scrambles the connection strengths, making them uncorrelated across the network. There’s no memory of where a link is located; each one is assigned independently, like rolling dice for every connection.<br>
<br>
This distinction is crucial because it affects how asymmetric different parts of the network are. Asymmetry here measures whether the flow or structure around a node looks the same in all directions or if it has a preferred orientation—like a one-way street versus a roundabout. The difference between the models shows up clearly in their asymmetry values, as seen in Fig. 5(c): the random links model (cyan) behaves very differently from the others, especially at various scales (represented by the degree $d$).<br>
<br>
Now let’s shift focus to **size distributions**. The **cumulative size distribution** $P(A \geq a)$ tells us the probability that a given node in the nesting tree is associated with an area $A$ greater than or equal to some value $a$. In simpler terms, imagine you&#x27;re exploring a river system: each junction in the river network defines a sub-basin, and the area of that sub-basin is what we&#x27;re measuring. $P(A \geq a)$ then answers the question: “What fraction of junctions control basins larger than size $a$?”<br>
<br>
We see these distributions plotted in Fig. 6(a) and Fig. 6(b,c). In Fig. 6(a), we compare the cumulative size distribution for several models: peaks (green), gradient (magenta), nested (blue), and random links (cyan). The gradient model follows a straight line with slope $1/2$ on a log-log plot—this is a signature of **scale invariance**, meaning the structure looks statistically similar no matter what scale you examine. The slight deviation at small $a$ is due to boundary effects—think of it like trying to measure the coastline of an island with a ruler that’s too big to capture tiny inlets.<br>
<br>
Now, look at the **peaks model**—its curve has kinks and changes in slope. These aren’t just random wiggles; they signal **qualitative shifts in the architecture** at certain scales. It’s like seeing a sudden change in building style as you drive from a downtown core into the suburbs—the structure isn’t smoothly evolving; something fundamental has changed.<br>
<br>
In contrast, both the **nested** and **random lines** models deviate significantly from the gradient model, indicating they don’t follow the same simple scaling law. But here’s a key insight: even though they don’t look like the gradient model, the nested models might still be **self-similar**, just in a more complex way. How can we test that?<br>
<br>
We do it by defining the **adjusted cumulative size distribution**: $a \cdot P(A \geq a)$. Why multiply by $a$? Here’s the intuition: if a system is truly self-similar, then $P(A \geq a) \sim 1/a$, meaning the chance of finding something larger than $a$ drops off exactly in proportion to $a$. So when we multiply $P(A \geq a)$ by $a$, we should get a constant—a flat line. Any deviation from flatness tells us the system isn’t perfectly scale-invariant.<br>
<br>
In Fig. 6(b), we plot $a \cdot P(A \geq a)$ for the **random links model**, and in Fig. 6(c), we do the same for the nested, nested5, nested10, and random lines models. For the self-similar models—especially the nested one—the curves fluctuate around a constant value, confirming their scale-invariant nature. The fact that they don’t form a perfectly flat line isn’t a flaw—it reflects the **topology of the iterative building block** used to construct the network. Just like how a repeating tile pattern might create subtle ripples in an otherwise uniform floor, the underlying generator of the nested network imprints a signature on the fluctuations.<br>
<br>
Moreover, the **periodicity** in the curve—those regular ripples—is incredibly informative. It can reveal the **characteristic size of the architectural unit** in the self-similar network. Think of it like hearing a repeating beat in music—the time between beats tells you the length of the musical phrase. Similarly, the spacing between peaks in $a \cdot P(A \geq a)$ tells us the “wavelength” of the network’s repeating structure.<br>
<br>
However, as we introduce more disorder—going from nested to nested5 to nested10—these ripples become less regular and eventually fade away entirely in the random lines model. The increasing disorder smears out the periodic signal, just like static blurring a clear radio station. By the time we reach the random lines model, there’s no discernible pattern left—no memory of the original architectural rhythm.<br>
