### Introduction<br>
<br>
Before we dive into the specifics, let&#x27;s set the stage with a fundamental question: *How do we &quot;see&quot; cancer inside the body?*<br>
<br>
Traditional imaging methods—like computed tomography (CT), ultrasound, or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—are **anatomically based**. They show us the *structure* of tissues: where a lump is, how big it is, and what it looks like. But here&#x27;s the catch: a tumor that is structurally present may not be functionally active, and a functionally aggressive tumor may be too small to see clearly on an anatomical scan.<br>
<br>
This is where **positron emission tomography (PET)** changes the game. PET is a **functional imaging technique**. Instead of just showing *where* something is, it shows *what it&#x27;s doing*. Specifically, PET with a tracer called **¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)**—a radioactive form of glucose—reveals where cells are consuming sugar at high rates. Since cancer cells are metabolically hyperactive (they burn sugar like a furnace), they light up brightly on an FDG-PET scan.<br>
<br>
The key insight here is that PET has demonstrated clear **advantages over anatomically based imaging modalities** in oncology, particularly when it comes to detecting malignant lesions. Why? Because a cancer cell&#x27;s *behavior* (its voracious appetite for glucose) often reveals its presence before its *structure* becomes visible or distinguishable from surrounding tissue.<br>
<br>
Now, here&#x27;s an exciting recent development. The introduction of **combined CT/PET systems** allows us to overlay—or **coregister**—the functional PET image with the anatomical CT image. Think of it like having a map (CT) and a live traffic report (PET) on the same screen. The CT tells you the roads and landmarks; the PET tells you where the &quot;traffic jams&quot; (active cancer cells) are. This fusion of information **will very likely improve accuracy** in detecting and characterizing disease.<br>
<br>
In this review, we will examine the role of PET and CT/PET specifically in **breast cancer**, with a focus on their use as **preoperative studies**—that is, before surgery, when critical decisions about staging and treatment planning are being made.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
### Methods<br>
<br>
In breast cancer, FDG-PET has been used for three main purposes: **detection** (finding the tumor), **staging** (determining how far it has spread), and **response monitoring** (checking whether treatment is working). One of its most important clinical applications is **defining the extent of recurrent or metastatic disease**—that is, determining whether the cancer has come back or spread to distant organs [1].<br>
<br>
We have systematically reviewed the evidence regarding the roles of PET and CT/PET as **preoperative studies** in breast cancer. The question we are asking is: *When a patient is about to undergo surgery for breast cancer, what can PET and CT/PET tell us that other tests cannot?*<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
### Results<br>
<br>
The review of the evidence shows that PET and CT/PET can contribute to patient diagnosis and management as preoperative studies in breast cancer in the following situations:<br>
<br>
#### 1. Detection of primary breast cancer<br>
<br>
When evaluating suspicious breast abnormalities, FDG-PET has a **sensitivity of 80–100%** and a **specificity of 75–100%**. Let&#x27;s unpack those numbers.<br>
<br>
- **Sensitivity** is the ability to correctly identify patients who *do* have the disease. A sensitivity of 80–100% means that out of 100 women with breast cancer, FDG-PET will correctly flag 80 to 100 of them.<br>
- **Specificity** is the ability to correctly identify patients who *do not* have the disease. A specificity of 75–100% means that out of 100 women without breast cancer, FDG-PET will correctly rule out 75 to 100 of them.<br>
<br>
These numbers sound impressive, right? So why is the role of PET in primary tumor detection **not clear** when compared with conventional imaging methods? The answer lies in a key limitation: **small tumors**. FDG-PET has difficulty detecting lesions smaller than a few millimeters, and it can also miss some slow-growing, low-grade tumors that simply don&#x27;t consume enough glucose to stand out. Meanwhile, conventional methods like mammography and ultrasound are excellent at detecting small structural abnormalities. So, while PET is powerful, it is not necessarily *better* than these established tools for the initial detection of a primary breast tumor. Its role here **remains to be determined** [1].<br>
<br>
#### 2. Prognostic value of FDG uptake in the primary tumor<br>
<br>
Here&#x27;s where PET offers something unique. Most studies suggest that the **level of FDG uptake** in primary breast tumors carries **clinical and biological information**. In other words, the brightness of the tumor on the PET scan is not just a pretty picture—it&#x27;s a biological signal.<br>
<br>
A **higher FDG uptake** is correlated with **more clinically aggressive tumors** [1]. Why would that be? Because aggressive tumors tend to be more metabolically active—they grow faster, divide more rapidly, and consume more glucose. The PET scan is essentially reading the tumor&#x27;s &quot;metabolic fingerprint.&quot;<br>
<br>
This information has practical value. It may help to **stratify patients according to prognosis and risk for recurrence**. For example, a patient with a very bright tumor might be flagged as higher risk, warranting more aggressive treatment. Conversely, a patient with a dim tumor might be a candidate for less intensive therapy. This is the beginning of **tailoring treatments to the individual patient**—a concept we now call personalized medicine.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
#### 3. Axillary node staging<br>
<br>
The axillary lymph nodes—located in the armpit—are the first place breast cancer typically spreads. Knowing whether these nodes are involved is critical for staging and treatment planning. Here, FDG-PET had a **sensitivity of 57–100%** and a **specificity of 66–100%**.<br>
<br>
But here&#x27;s the crucial caveat: **FDG-PET underestimates the number of tumor-involved nodes** compared with pathologic evaluation from conventional dissection. In other words, the PET scan may show fewer positive nodes than actually exist. Why? Because PET can miss **microscopic nodal involvement**—tiny clusters of cancer cells too small to produce a detectable metabolic signal.<br>
<br>
This is a critical limitation. Even microscopic nodal involvement may be important for prognosis and treatment planning. Therefore, **FDG-PET should not replace axillary node sampling for routine staging of the axilla** [1]. The standard procedure—sentinel lymph node biopsy or axillary dissection—remains the gold standard.<br>
<br>
However, FDG-PET may be **complementary** to sentinel lymph node mapping and other standard axillary procedures. Specifically, it can be useful in patients with **more advanced tumors** or **equivocally palpable axillary nodes**—that is, nodes that feel suspicious on physical exam but are not clearly abnormal. In these cases, PET can provide additional information to guide the surgeon.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
#### 4. Detection of locoregional and distant metastases<br>
<br>
This is where PET truly shines. Let&#x27;s look at the mediastinum—the area in the chest between the lungs that contains the heart, esophagus, and lymph nodes.<br>
<br>
For detecting **nodal involvement in the mediastinum**, functional imaging with FDG-PET is **more accurate than CT**. The sensitivity of FDG-PET was **significantly higher (85%)** than CT (50%), with nearly the same specificity (90% for FDG-PET versus 83% for CT). This means that PET is much better at *finding* involved nodes (85% vs. 50%) while maintaining a similar ability to *rule out* uninvolved nodes.<br>
<br>
Why is PET so much better here? Because CT relies on size criteria—a node is considered suspicious if it is enlarged. But a normal-sized node can contain cancer, and an enlarged node can be benign (e.g., due to inflammation). PET, on the other hand, detects *metabolic activity*, which is a more direct indicator of cancer.<br>
<br>
Regarding the detection of **distant metastases**—cancer that has spread to other organs like the liver, bones, or lungs—FDG-PET can accurately detect sites of distant disease with a **sensitivity of 80–97%** and a **specificity of 75–94%** [1]. This is critical for staging, because the presence of distant metastases changes the treatment approach entirely (from curative surgery to systemic therapy).<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
#### 5. Evaluation of therapy response<br>
<br>
In **locally advanced breast cancer (LABC)**, the tumor is large and has spread to nearby tissues but not to distant organs. In this setting, patients often receive **neoadjuvant chemotherapy**—chemotherapy given *before* surgery to shrink the tumor and make it operable.<br>
<br>
The problem is that assessing response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy with conventional imaging methods is **often inaccurate or slow**. Why? Because conventional methods rely on **morphological criteria**—they measure changes in tumor size. But a tumor may be dying (responding to treatment) without shrinking much yet, or it may be growing while appearing stable in size. Size is a lagging indicator.<br>
<br>
Initial studies have shown the utility of FDG-PET in the evaluation of treatment response, specifically in its ability to **discriminate responders from nonresponders more accurately and earlier** than conventional imaging methods [1]. The key insight is that **changes in FDG uptake**—not just tumor size—can predict the pathological response. After a single course of chemotherapy, changes in FDG uptake can predict the pathological response in primary LABC tumors [2,3]. Histopathological response could be predicted with an **accuracy of 88–91%** after the first and second course of chemotherapy [3].<br>
<br>
This is a game-changer. Instead of waiting weeks or months to see if a tumor shrinks, we can use PET to see if the tumor&#x27;s metabolism is *slowing down*—a much earlier and more direct sign of response.<br>
<br>
Other PET tracers may also be used in the evaluation of the primary tumor. Preliminary results suggest that applying PET in this way may help to identify **physiologic manifestations of drug resistance**, which would help to tailor systemic therapy [1]. In other words, PET might tell us not just *whether* the tumor is responding, but *why* it might not be responding—allowing us to switch to a different drug sooner.<br>
<br>
Preliminary data also suggest that FDG-PET may be useful in the assessment of **sites of disease other than the primary tumor** for monitoring response to chemotherapy in advanced breast cancer. ## Introduction<br>
<br>
Imagine you&#x27;re trying to find a hidden fire in a building. You could look at blueprints (anatomy) to see where things *should* be—but that won’t tell you if something is actively burning. Now, what if you had a thermal camera that detects heat (function)? That’s the key idea behind **positron emission tomography (PET)** using a special sugar-like molecule called **¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)**.<br>
<br>
Here’s how it works: cancer cells are like overactive engines—they consume energy much faster than normal cells. Since they love sugar for fuel, we give the patient a radioactive version of glucose—FDG. The cancer cells greedily take it up, and the PET scanner detects the radiation emitted, lighting up these “hot spots” of metabolic activity. This makes PET a **functional imaging technique**, meaning it shows *what tissues are doing*, not just what they look like.<br>
<br>
In contrast, traditional imaging methods like **computed tomography (CT)** show detailed *anatomical structure*—the shape, size, and location of organs and tissues. But sometimes, a tumor might look small on a CT scan yet be biologically aggressive, or a mass might appear suspicious but turn out to be benign. That’s where combining both types of information becomes powerful.<br>
<br>
Enter **combined CT/PET systems**—a technological breakthrough that merges the best of both worlds. These machines take a CT scan and a PET scan in one session, then precisely overlay the images. The result? Doctors can see *exactly* where increased metabolic activity (from PET) occurs within the detailed anatomy (from CT). This coregistration—aligning functional and structural data—greatly improves diagnostic accuracy. Think of it like GPS navigation: the map is the CT (anatomy), and the moving traffic signals are the PET (metabolism). Together, they give a complete picture.<br>
<br>
So, what does this mean for breast cancer? This review explores how PET and CT/PET can be used **before surgery**—as preoperative tools—to improve diagnosis, staging, and treatment planning.<br>
<br>
## Methods<br>
<br>
In breast cancer care, FDG-PET has been studied across several critical areas: finding the original tumor, determining how far the disease has spread (staging), and monitoring how well treatments are working. One of its most valuable uses so far is in identifying **recurrent or metastatic disease**—cancer that has come back or spread to other parts of the body—where conventional imaging often falls short.<br>
<br>
For this review, we examined the existing scientific evidence on the role of PET and CT/PET specifically as **preoperative studies** in breast cancer. That means we focused on how these tools help guide decisions *before* surgery—such as whether the cancer is localized, if lymph nodes are involved, or if distant spread has already occurred, which could change the entire treatment plan.<br>
<br>
## Results<br>
<br>
Our analysis shows that PET and CT/PET can significantly enhance patient management before surgery in several key clinical scenarios:<br>
<br>
### 1. Detection of primary breast cancer<br>
<br>
When doctors find a suspicious lump or abnormality on a mammogram or ultrasound, they need to determine whether it’s cancerous. FDG-PET can help here because malignant tumors typically show high metabolic activity.<br>
<br>
Studies show that FDG-PET has a **sensitivity of 80–100%**—meaning it correctly identifies 80 to 100 out of every 100 actual cancers—and a **specificity of 75–100%**, meaning it correctly rules out cancer in 75 to 100 out of every 100 benign cases.<br>
<br>
But here’s the catch: while PET is good at detecting larger, more aggressive tumors, it often misses small tumors (&lt;1 cm) or certain subtypes like **lobular carcinoma**, which tend to have lower metabolic activity. Also, some benign conditions—like infections or inflammation—can also take up FDG, leading to false positives.<br>
<br>
Compared to standard imaging (mammography, ultrasound, MRI), PET doesn’t yet offer a clear advantage for routine detection of the primary tumor. So, its role here remains uncertain and is still under investigation. It’s not currently recommended as a first-line screening tool, but it may be helpful in complex cases where other tests are inconclusive.<br>
<br>
&gt; 🔍 **Learner’s Insight:** Why not use PET for everyone? Because it’s expensive, involves radiation exposure, and isn’t always better than existing tools for early detection. Medicine aims to use the *right tool for the right patient*—not the most advanced one.<br>
<br>
### 2. Prognostic value of FDG uptake in primary tumor<br>
<br>
Here’s a fascinating insight: how brightly a tumor &quot;lights up&quot; on PET isn’t just about presence—it tells us something about *behavior*. The amount of FDG uptake is measured by a number called **SUVmax (Standardized Uptake Value)**—essentially, a quantitative measure of how metabolically active the tumor is.<br>
<br>
Multiple studies show that **higher FDG uptake correlates with more aggressive tumor features**: faster cell division (high Ki-67), lack of hormone receptors (ER/PR negative), HER2 positivity, and higher tumor grade. In short, the brighter the signal, the more likely the cancer is to grow quickly and spread.<br>
<br>
This isn’t just academic—it has real clinical value. By measuring FDG uptake, doctors can **stratify patients by risk**. Someone with high SUVmax might benefit from more aggressive treatment upfront, while someone with low uptake might avoid unnecessary chemotherapy. This moves us toward **personalized medicine**, tailoring therapy based on the biology of the individual tumor.<br>
<br>
&gt; 🧠 **Analogy:** Think of SUVmax like a car’s RPM gauge. A high reading doesn’t just mean the engine is running—it suggests it’s revving hard, possibly racing. That tells you something about how the driver (the tumor) is behaving.<br>
<br>
### 3. Axillary node staging<br>
<br>
One of the most important factors in breast cancer prognosis is whether cancer has spread to the **axillary lymph nodes** (under the arm). This guides decisions about surgery, radiation, and systemic therapy.<br>
<br>
Traditionally, surgeons perform **sentinel lymph node biopsy** or full axillary dissection to check for microscopic disease. But can PET replace this invasive step?<br>
<br>
The data says: **not yet**. FDG-PET has variable sensitivity—ranging from **57% to 100%**—and specificity from **66% to 100%**. The wide range reflects differences in tumor size, node size, and scanner resolution. More importantly, PET often **misses micrometastases**—tiny clusters of cancer cells that are too small to generate a detectable signal.<br>
<br>
Because even a single involved node affects prognosis and treatment, **FDG-PET cannot replace pathological evaluation** of lymph nodes. Relying solely on PET could lead to undertreatment.<br>
<br>
However, PET can still play a **complementary role**. For example, in patients with **clinically suspicious or palpable nodes**, a positive PET scan can confirm involvement and help plan surgery. It may also help identify unexpected nodal disease outside the usual drainage areas.<br>
<br>
&gt; ⚠️ **Common Misconception Alert:** A negative PET scan in the axilla does *not* rule out nodal involvement. Always correlate with clinical and pathological findings.<br>
<br>
### 4. Detection of locoregional and distant metastases<br>
<br>
This is where PET truly shines. While CT looks for structural changes—like enlarged lymph nodes or liver lesions—PET detects abnormal metabolism, often *before* structural changes occur.<br>
<br>
For **mediastinal lymph nodes** (in the chest), PET is far more sensitive than CT: **85% vs. 50%**, respectively. Both have similar specificity (~90% for PET, 83% for CT). This means PET is much better at catching early nodal spread in the chest, which can drastically alter staging and treatment.<br>
<br>
When it comes to **distant metastases**—cancer that has spread to bones, liver, lungs, or distant lymph nodes—FDG-PET is highly accurate. Studies report **sensitivity of 80–97%** and **specificity of 75–94%**. This makes it an excellent tool for **whole-body staging** in one scan.<br>
<br>
Consider this: a patient thought to have early-stage cancer based on CT might be found to have unsuspected bone or liver metastases on PET. That changes everything—from curative surgery to systemic therapy as the main approach.<br>
<br>
&gt; 🌐 **Big Picture Connection:** This ability to detect occult (hidden) metastases makes PET invaluable in **re-staging** patients, especially those with locally advanced or recurrent disease. It prevents futile surgeries and ensures patients get the most appropriate treatment from the start.<br>
<br>
### 5. Evaluation of therapy response<br>
<br>
Treating **locally advanced breast cancer (LABC)** often begins with **neoadjuvant chemotherapy**—chemo given *before* surgery to shrink the tumor. Traditionally, doctors assess response by measuring tumor size on physical exam or imaging (like ultrasound or MRI). But size changes lag behind biological changes—sometimes by weeks.<br>
<br>
Wouldn’t it be better to know *early* whether the treatment is working?<br>
<br>
Enter FDG-PET. Because it measures metabolic activity, it can detect changes **within days** of starting chemo. If the tumor stops consuming FDG, it’s likely responding—even if it hasn’t shrunk yet.<br>
<br>
Multiple studies show that **a drop in FDG uptake after just one cycle of chemotherapy** can predict whether the tumor will have a good **pathological response** (i.e., little or no cancer left at surgery). Accuracy ranges from **88% to 91%** after the first or second cycle.<br>
<br>
This is a game-changer. Imagine being able to switch non-responders to a different drug regimen *early*, avoiding months of ineffective, toxic treatment.<br>
<br>
Beyond FDG, researchers are exploring other **PET tracers** that target different aspects of tumor biology—like cell proliferation or hormone receptor expression. These could help detect **drug resistance** earlier and guide more precise systemic therapies.<br>
