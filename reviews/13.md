<td valign="top"><div>## The Effect of Soluble Solids on Flow Activation Energy<br>
<br>
When we want to understand how the concentration of dissolved solids (the soluble solids content, measured in °Brix) affects the flow activation energy (Ea) of enzyme-clarified Indian gooseberry juice, we need a mathematical way to describe that relationship. The researchers used two candidate equations to model this effect: a **power law** and an **exponential** equation.<br>
<br>
**Power law:**<br>
Ea = a (C)b, (3)<br>
<br>
**Exponential type:**<br>
Ea = a exp (b C), (4)<br>
<br>
Here, *a* and *b* are constants that we will estimate from the data. *Ea* is the flow activation energy (in J mol⁻¹), and *C* is the soluble solid content (in °Brix). <br>
<br>
**What is flow activation energy?** Think of it as the energy barrier that molecules must overcome to start flowing. A higher activation energy means the juice is more sensitive to temperature changes—it thickens more dramatically when cooled and thins more dramatically when heated.<br>
<br>
**What is a power law vs. an exponential?** <br>
- In a power law (Eq. 3), the variable *C* is raised to a power *b*. This means that as *C* increases, *Ea* grows at a rate that depends on *C* itself. <br>
- In an exponential (Eq. 4), the variable *C* sits in the exponent of the natural number *e*. This means that *Ea* grows at a rate proportional to its current value—a faster-than-polynomial growth.<br>
<br>
**Our goal:** We want to see which of these two mathematical shapes best describes the actual data. To do this, we fit both equations to the measured values of *Ea* at different concentrations. The method of least squares is used—this is a standard technique that finds the values of *a* and *b* that minimize the total squared difference between the model&#x27;s predictions and the actual data points. The results are shown in Table 4.<br>
<br>
**The verdict:** The exponential model (Eq. 4) wins. The correlation coefficient (R) for the exponential model is high (R &gt; 0.9954), while the power law model gives a lower correlation (R &lt; 0.9875). <br>
<br>
**What does a correlation coefficient tell us?** It measures how well the model&#x27;s predictions match the actual data. A value close to 1 means a nearly perfect fit. So, the exponential model explains the variation in *Ea* with concentration much better than the power law does. This is a strong signal that the underlying physics is exponential in nature.<br>
<br>

## Why Does Viscosity Increase with Soluble Solids?<br>
<br>
Now, let&#x27;s shift our focus to the viscosity of the juice. The concentration of both soluble and insoluble solids has a strong, **non-linear** effect on the viscosity of Newtonian fluids (Krokida et al., 2001). <br>
<br>
**What is a Newtonian fluid?** A fluid where the viscosity is constant regardless of the shear rate—it doesn&#x27;t matter how fast you stir it, the resistance to flow stays the same. Water and this clarified juice are good examples.<br>
<br>
**The mechanism:** As the soluble solids concentration increases, the viscosity of the juice increases. Why? Because more dissolved sugar molecules mean more hydrated molecules—water molecules cling to the sugar molecules via hydrogen bonding with their hydroxyl groups. This effectively makes the particles larger and more numerous, increasing the internal friction and thus the viscosity.<br>
<br>
**A key observation:** Regardless of temperature, an increase in soluble solids content always increases viscosity. This is because the higher concentration promotes aggregation between particles, which in turn increases the interaction between them. More particles bumping into each other means more resistance to flow.<br>
<br>

<td valign="top"><div>## The effect of soluble solids on flow activation energy<br>
<br>
When we heat or cool a liquid like fruit juice, its thickness—what scientists call *viscosity*—changes. But how easily it flows when heated also depends on what’s dissolved inside it. In the case of enzyme-clarified Indian gooseberry juice, one key factor is the amount of **soluble solids**, which is mostly sugar and other plant compounds. These are measured in degrees Brix (°Bx), a unit that tells us how much dissolved material is in the juice.<br>
<br>
Now, when we want to understand how temperature affects the flow of this juice, we use a concept called **flow activation energy**, or $ E_a $. Think of $ E_a $ like a “flow barrier”: the higher the $ E_a $, the more energy (in the form of heat) you need to get the molecules moving and the juice flowing. It’s similar to how much effort you’d need to push a heavy box across a rough floor—the rougher the floor (or the thicker the liquid), the more energy you need.<br>
<br>
So, how do the dissolved sugars affect this energy barrier? Researchers tested two mathematical models to describe how $ E_a $ changes as the concentration of soluble solids increases. These models help us predict the behavior of the juice under different conditions—essential for designing industrial processes like pasteurization or bottling.<br>
<br>
### Two models for flow activation energy<br>
<br>
The first model is the **power law** equation:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
E_a = a (C)^b, \tag{3}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
The second is the **exponential-type** equation:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
E_a = a \exp(b C), \tag{4}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Let’s unpack these.<br>
<br>
- $ E_a $: This is the **flow activation energy**, measured in joules per mole (J mol⁻¹). It tells us how sensitive the flow of the juice is to temperature changes.<br>
- $ C $: This stands for **soluble solids content**, given in °Brix. It’s the independent variable—the thing we’re changing to see how $ E_a $ responds.<br>
- $ a $ and $ b $: These are **fitting parameters**, or constants that are adjusted so the equation best matches the experimental data. They don’t have a direct physical meaning on their own, but their values tell us about the strength and nature of the relationship between concentration and energy.<br>
<br>
Now, why choose one model over the other? That’s where data fitting comes in.<br>
<br>
Using the **method of least squares**, scientists plugged in the measured values of $ E_a $ and $ C $ to find the best possible values for $ a $ and $ b $ in both equations. This method minimizes the total difference between the predicted values from the equation and the actual lab measurements—like drawing the smoothest line through scattered data points.<br>
<br>
The results, summarized in Table 4 (not shown here), revealed something important: the **exponential model (Equation 4)** described the data better than the power law model (Equation 3). How do we know? By looking at the **correlation coefficient**, denoted $ R $, which measures how closely the model matches the data. A value of $ R = 1 $ means a perfect fit.<br>
<br>
- For the exponential model: $ R &gt; 0.9954 $<br>
- For the power law model: $ R &lt; 0.9875 $<br>
<br>
Since the exponential model has a higher $ R $, it captures the trend more accurately. This suggests that the relationship between soluble solids and flow activation energy isn’t just a simple power relationship—it grows faster, in a way that an exponential function can better describe. In practical terms, this means that each additional increment of sugar has a *compounding* effect on the energy needed to make the juice flow.<br>
<br>
### Why does concentration affect viscosity and flow?<br>
<br>
But why does adding more soluble solids make the juice harder to flow in the first place?<br>
<br>
Even before we consider temperature, increasing the concentration of soluble solids **increases the viscosity** of the juice. This happens because more dissolved molecules—like sugars and organic acids—interact with water through **hydrogen bonding**. These molecules have hydroxyl (–OH) groups that attract water molecules, forming a kind of &quot;hydration shell&quot; around them. As more of these hydrated molecules are packed into the juice, they get in each other’s way, making the liquid thicker.<br>
<br>
Moreover, higher concentration leads to **increased particle aggregation**. The dissolved molecules start to cluster together, increasing the number and strength of interactions between them. This network of interactions resists flow, raising viscosity. And this effect happens **regardless of temperature**—though temperature can reduce viscosity by giving molecules more energy to break free from these interactions.<br>
<br>
This strong, **non-linear** effect of solids on viscosity is well-documented in Newtonian fluids (fluids whose viscosity doesn’t change with applied force), as noted by Krokida et al. (2001).<br>
<br>
### Modeling viscosity directly<br>
<br>
Since viscosity itself is so sensitive to concentration, researchers also used two similar equations to model how **viscosity ($ \eta $)** changes with soluble solids content at fixed temperatures:<br>
<br>
**Power law model:**<br>
$$<br>
\eta = a (C)^b, \tag{5}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
**Exponential-type model:**<br>
$$<br>
\eta = a \exp(b C). \tag{6}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Here, $ \eta $ (eta) is the viscosity in millipascal-seconds (mPa·s), and the rest of the symbols mean the same as before.<br>
<br>
Again, the parameters $ a $ and $ b $ were estimated using the **least squares method** at different temperatures, and the results are shown in Table 5. Once more, the **exponential model outperformed** the power law model. The correlation coefficients ($ R $) for the exponential model ranged from **0.9887 to 0.9951**, indicating an excellent fit across all temperatures.<br>
<br>
This consistency across models and conditions tells us something powerful: the way dissolved solids affect the physical behavior of fruit juices isn’t linear or simple—it’s **exponentially sensitive**. Small increases in sugar content can lead to disproportionately large increases in viscosity and flow resistance.<br>
<br>
This insight is crucial for food engineers. For example, when designing heat exchangers or pumps, knowing that viscosity rises exponentially with concentration helps in selecting the right equipment and energy inputs. It also explains why concentrated juices behave so differently from diluted ones, even at the same temperature.<br>
