Now we turn to a crucial question: does the *context* in which a firm operates change the payoff of using sophisticated capital budgeting practices? Specifically, we ask whether social uncertainty—the unpredictability of the social, political, or regulatory environment—acts as a *moderator*. <br>
<br>
Let’s unpack what “moderator” means here. A moderator is a variable that changes the strength (or even the direction) of the relationship between two other variables. In our case, the relationship of interest is between sophisticated capital budgeting practices (our predictor) and Tobin-q (our outcome, a measure of firm performance). The moderator is social uncertainty. The hypothesis we are testing is straightforward: the positive link between sophisticated capital budgeting practices and Tobin-q should be *weaker* for firms facing higher social uncertainty than for firms facing lower social uncertainty. Why might this be? Think of it this way: if the environment is highly unpredictable, even the best-laid financial plans may be rendered obsolete by sudden shifts in policy, regulation, or social conditions. Thus, the value of sophisticated planning is diminished.<br>
<br>
To test this, we run a moderated regression analysis. The key term to look for is the *interaction term*—the product of the two predictors (Sophisticated CBP × Social uncertainty). If this interaction term is statistically significant, it tells us that the effect of one variable depends on the level of the other. The results are shown in Table 6.<br>

## 4.4.1 Social Uncertainty as a Moderator of the Relationship between Sophisticated Capital Budgeting Practices and Tobin_q<br>
<br>
Let’s step into the shoes of a researcher trying to understand what drives firm performance—and more specifically, whether using advanced financial tools actually helps companies create value. One common measure of firm performance is **Tobin’s q**, which compares a company’s market value to the replacement cost of its assets. A Tobin’s q greater than 1 suggests the market believes the firm is creating value beyond its physical assets—perhaps through innovation, brand strength, or smart management.<br>
<br>
Now, many firms use what researchers call **sophisticated capital budgeting practices (CBP)**—tools like discounted cash flow analysis, real options valuation, or scenario planning—to decide which projects to invest in. The idea is that better decision-making tools should lead to better investment choices, which in turn should boost firm value (i.e., increase Tobin’s q). And indeed, prior research often finds a positive link between these sophisticated methods and performance.<br>
<br>
But here’s a critical question: **Does this benefit hold true under all conditions?** Or could external factors weaken the payoff from using advanced tools?<br>
<br>
This section investigates one such factor: **social uncertainty**. What do we mean by that? Imagine a society where people distrust institutions, political instability is high, or social norms are shifting rapidly. In such environments, even the best financial models may struggle to predict outcomes because the &quot;rules of the game&quot; keep changing. Consumer behavior, government policy, or labor relations might be unpredictable—not due to economic cycles, but because of deeper societal turbulence.<br>
<br>
So the hypothesis here is this:  <br>
&gt; *The positive effect of sophisticated capital budgeting practices on firm value (Tobin’s q) will be weaker when firms operate in contexts of high social uncertainty.*<br>
<br>
In other words, **social uncertainty acts as a moderator**—a factor that changes the strength (or even direction) of a relationship between two other variables. If you think of the relationship between sophisticated CBP and Tobin’s q as an engine that generates value, social uncertainty is like thick air or rough terrain—it doesn’t stop the engine, but it makes it work less efficiently.<br>
<br>
To test this idea, the researchers used a statistical technique called **moderated regression analysis**. This allows them to see not just whether sophisticated CBP predicts Tobin’s q, but whether that prediction gets stronger or weaker depending on the level of social uncertainty.<br>
<br>
The results are summarized in **Table 6**.<br>
<br>
### Table 6. Social uncertainty as a moderator between sophisticated capital budgeting practices and Tobin_q  <br>
<br>

<br>
Let’s unpack this table step by step, because each row tells us something important.<br>
<br>
First, the **constant** (or intercept) is not statistically significant (p = .9844), which means we can’t say the baseline Tobin’s q differs significantly from zero when all predictors are at their mean values. That’s fine—it’s not the focus here.<br>
<br>
Second, **social uncertainty alone** has a negative and highly significant association with Tobin’s q (b = –0.40, p &lt; .001). This suggests that, on average, firms in more socially uncertain environments tend to have lower market valuations, independent of their budgeting practices. That makes intuitive sense—investors may discount the value of firms operating in unstable societies.<br>
<br>
Third, **sophisticated capital budgeting practices alone** show a strong positive relationship with Tobin’s q (b = 0.41, p &lt; .001). So, all else being equal, firms using advanced tools do appear to create more value. This aligns with conventional wisdom in finance.<br>
<br>
Now comes the key part: the **interaction term**—*Sophisticated CBP × Social Uncertainty*—which tests our moderation hypothesis. The coefficient here is **b = –0.32**, and it&#x27;s statistically significant (p = .0278). A negative interaction coefficient means that as social uncertainty increases, the positive effect of sophisticated CBP on Tobin’s q **decreases**.<br>
<br>
Think of it like this:  <br>
Imagine two firms with identical levels of sophisticated capital budgeting practices—one in a stable society, one in a volatile one. The firm in the stable environment gets a bigger boost in market value from using advanced tools than the one in the turbulent setting. The tools still help, but their impact is dampened by the surrounding chaos.<br>
<br>
To visualize how this moderation works, the authors provide **Figure 1**.<br>
<br>
### Figure 1. Graphical presentation of social uncertainty as a moderator between sophisticated capital budgeting practices and Tobin_q<br>
<br>
In this figure, the x-axis shows levels of **sophisticated capital budgeting practices**, and the y-axis shows the predicted value of **Tobin’s q**. But instead of one line, there are **three separate regression lines**, each representing a different level of social uncertainty:<br>
<br>
1. **Low social uncertainty** (defined as one standard deviation below the mean; value = –1.000)  <br>
2. **Average social uncertainty** (at the mean; value = 0, due to centering)  <br>
3. **High social uncertainty** (at +0.5405 standard deviations above the mean)<br>
<br>
Centering the variables (subtracting the mean) helps avoid multicollinearity and makes the interpretation of the interaction clearer.<br>
<br>
Now, look at the slopes of these three lines. A steeper slope means a stronger relationship between sophisticated CBP and Tobin’s q.<br>
<br>
- At **low social uncertainty**, the relationship is strongest: **b = 0.73**, 95% CI [0.32, 1.14], p &lt; .05. That’s a large and significant effect—the firm gains substantial value from using advanced tools.<br>
- At **average social uncertainty**, the effect is still positive and significant, but smaller: **b = 0.41**, 95% CI [0.24, 0.58], p &lt; .05. This matches the main effect from the regression table.<br>
- At **high social uncertainty**, the effect remains positive and significant, but even weaker: **b = 0.24**, 95% CI [0.10, 0.38], p &lt; .05.<br>
<br>
So, while sophisticated capital budgeting practices are always beneficial across all levels of social uncertainty, **their value-creating power diminishes as social uncertainty rises**.<br>
<br>
This pattern is exactly what a **negative moderating effect** looks like: the same managerial practice yields diminishing returns when the external social environment becomes less predictable.<br>
<br>
It’s a bit like driving a high-performance car. On a smooth, clear highway (low uncertainty), you can really take advantage of the engine’s power. But on a bumpy, foggy mountain road (high uncertainty), even the best car can’t go fast or safely—no matter how skilled the driver.<br>
<br>
Thus, the takeaway is not that sophisticated tools are useless in uncertain times, but that **their effectiveness depends on context**. Managers shouldn’t assume that adopting advanced financial techniques automatically translates into higher firm value—especially if they’re operating in socially volatile environments.<br>
<br>
These findings also suggest that improving institutional stability and reducing societal unpredictability could amplify the benefits of good financial management practices. In that sense, the study bridges corporate finance and broader socio-institutional theory.<br>
<br>
Overall, the results confirm that **social uncertainty significantly weakens the positive relationship between sophisticated capital budgeting practices and Tobin’s q**, providing strong empirical support for the moderation hypothesis.<br>
