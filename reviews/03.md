<br>
Before we go further, let’s pause to make sure we’re on the same page about what “cleared from the bloodstream” means. In this experiment, the researchers were measuring the number of live pneumococci (the bacteria that cause pneumonia and other infections) circulating in the blood of mice at various time points after infection. The “limit of detection” here was **10³ CFU/mL of blood**—that is, if there were fewer than 1,000 bacterial colonies per milliliter of blood, the assay couldn’t reliably detect them. So when we say a strain was “cleared,” we mean that the bacterial count dropped below this threshold, not necessarily that every single bacterium was eliminated. It’s a practical, measurable definition of clearance.  <br>
<br>
Now, here’s the key insight that ties all these observations together: **the number of pneumococci in the blood at 30 hours post-infection was strongly and positively correlated with the number of pneumococci recovered from the heart** of the same mice (R² = 0.852; P &lt; 0.0001). Let’s unpack that.  <br>
<br>
- **R² = 0.852** means that 85.2% of the variation in heart bacterial counts can be explained by the variation in blood bacterial counts. In plain terms, if you know how many bacteria are in the blood, you can predict with high confidence how many will be in the heart.  <br>
- **P &lt; 0.0001** means the probability that this correlation happened by random chance is less than 1 in 10,000. This is a very strong statistical signal.  <br>
<br>
So, what does this tell us? It suggests that the bacteria don’t just magically appear in the heart—they need to be present in the bloodstream in large numbers first. The bloodstream is like a highway, and the heart is a destination. If the highway is empty, few cars (bacteria) reach the destination.  <br>
<br>
But the story doesn’t stop there. There was also a **weaker but still positive correlation** between the number of bacteria in the heart and the serum levels of **troponin-I** (Fig 1C). Troponin-I is a protein that is normally found inside heart muscle cells. When heart muscle cells are damaged or die, they release troponin into the blood. So measuring troponin-I in the blood is like listening for a distress signal from the heart. The more bacteria in the heart, the more damage, and the louder the distress signal.  <br>
<br>
Thus, the ability of *S. pneumoniae* to invade the heart and trigger the release of troponin seemed to be **dependent upon the number of bacteria in the bloodstream**. This is a classic “dose-response” relationship: more bacteria in the blood → more bacteria in the heart → more heart damage.  <br>
<br>
To validate this, hearts from infected mice were sectioned and invaded pneumococci were visualized by immunofluorescence against capsule (S1 Fig). Only mice with high-levels of bacteremia showed bacteria in the heart. This is a direct visual confirmation of the statistical correlation: if you look at the heart tissue, you only see bacteria in the mice that had high blood counts.  <br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
Now, the researchers asked a natural follow-up question: **If we artificially increase the bacteremia, will we see more cardiac damage?** This is a classic experimental strategy—take a variable you think is important (blood bacterial load) and manipulate it directly to see if it causes the predicted effect.  <br>
<br>
They chose the strain **CDC7F:2617–97**, which had shown only mid-grade bacteremia and was cleared by 16 hours. They infected mice with a **100-fold higher dose** (10⁵ CFU instead of 10³ CFU). The logic: if the strain’s low cardiac invasion was simply due to low blood counts, then giving a higher dose should produce higher blood counts, and thus more heart damage.  <br>
<br>
But here’s the surprise: **the bacteremia was not more severe**. The average blood titer at euthanasia was 2.48 × 10³ CFU/mL, which is essentially the same as the lower-dose group (P = 0.2251). This is a crucial negative result. It tells us that simply giving more bacteria doesn’t necessarily lead to higher blood counts—the host’s immune system, or the bacteria’s own growth characteristics, may limit the peak bacteremia.  <br>
<br>
What about cardiac involvement?  <br>
- With the low dose (10³ CFU), only **2 of 6 mice (33.3%)** had bacteria in the heart.  <br>
- With the high dose (10⁵ CFU), **3 of 5 mice (60%)** had detectable bacteria in the heart.  <br>
<br>
So there was a **modest increase** in the proportion of mice with cardiac involvement. However, when they compared the actual bacterial burden in the heart between the two groups, the difference was **not statistically significant** (P = 0.8701). This means that while more mice had bacteria in the heart, the amount of bacteria in those hearts was not higher.  <br>
<br>
This is a subtle but important distinction. It’s like saying: “More people visited the store, but they didn’t buy more items.” The invasion of the heart is not simply a linear function of the dose.  <br>
<br>
Next, they tried another approach to boost disease severity: **depleting neutrophils**. Neutrophils are a type of white blood cell that are often the first responders to bacterial infections. They are like the emergency medical team that rushes to the site of an injury. The researchers used an antibody against **Ly6G**, a protein on the surface of neutrophils, to remove them from the mice. This also depletes, to a lesser extent, monocytes and macrophages (other immune cells).  <br>
<br>
The idea was: if the immune system is holding CDC7F:2617–97 in check, removing the neutrophils should allow the bacteria to flourish and cause more damage. But again, the result was negative: there was no enhancement of disease severity (P = 0.8485). In fact, only **1 of 5 mice** had pneumococci detectable in the blood at 26 hours post-infection (2.1 × 10⁴ CFU/mL), and **none** had detectable levels of troponin-I.  <br>
<br>
So even without neutrophils, this strain still didn’t cause significant heart damage. This suggests that CDC7F:2617–97 is attenuated in some other way—it’s not just being held back by the immune system.  <br>
<br>
To rule out one possible mechanism, they tested whether the strains were susceptible to **complement-mediated killing**. Complement is a set of proteins in the blood that can punch holes in bacteria and mark them for destruction. If CDC7F:2617–97 were highly susceptible to complement, that would explain why it’s cleared so quickly. But the results (S3 Fig) showed that **none of the strains tested were susceptible** to complement-mediated killing after incubation in whole unfixed human serum for two hours. This means the attenuation of CDC7F:2617–97 is due to something else—perhaps a metabolic deficiency, a lack of certain surface proteins, or an inability to survive in the heart tissue itself.  <br>

This cutoff is important because it defines the sensitivity of our measurements; just because bacteria aren’t detected doesn’t necessarily mean they’re completely absent, only that their numbers are too low for our tools to reliably pick up.<br>
<br>
Critically, the number of *S. pneumoniae* (pneumococci) present in the blood of mice at 30 hours post-infection showed a strong, positive correlation (R² = 0.852; *P* &lt; 0.0001) with the number recovered from perfused and homogenized heart tissue from the same animals (Fig 1B). Let’s unpack what this means. The R² value—called the coefficient of determination—tells us how well the variation in one variable (here, heart bacterial load) can be explained by variation in another (blood bacterial load). An R² of 0.852 is quite high, suggesting that about 85% of the differences in heart infection levels across mice can be directly linked to how many bacteria were circulating in their blood. The extremely low *P*-value (*P* &lt; 0.0001) indicates this relationship is highly unlikely to be due to random chance—it’s a real, statistically significant trend.<br>
<br>
In turn, there was a weaker but still positive correlation between the levels of bacteria found in the heart and the serum levels of troponin-I (Fig 1C). Troponin-I is a protein normally found inside heart muscle cells (cardiomyocytes); when these cells are damaged, troponin leaks into the bloodstream. Clinically, elevated troponin-I is a key biomarker used to diagnose heart injury, such as during a heart attack. So, detecting higher troponin-I in infected mice suggests that the presence of bacteria in the heart is causing actual structural damage to cardiac tissue.<br>
<br>
Thus, the ability of *S. pneumoniae* to invade the heart and trigger the release of troponin appears to depend on the number of bacteria in the bloodstream. In other words, more bacteria in the blood → greater chance of heart invasion → more heart cell damage → higher troponin-I levels. This paints a clear picture of a dose-dependent process: the severity of cardiac involvement escalates with the intensity of bacteremia.<br>
<br>
To validate this idea, hearts from infected mice were sectioned and examined using immunofluorescence staining targeting the bacterial capsule—a sugar-coated outer layer unique to *S. pneumoniae*. This technique allows us to literally *see* where the bacteria are located within the heart tissue. Importantly, only mice that had high levels of bacteremia (i.e., lots of bacteria in the blood) showed visible pneumococci in their heart tissue. Mice with low or undetectable bacteremia had no bacteria in the heart. This visual confirmation strengthens the statistical findings: bloodstream infection must reach a certain threshold before the bacteria can breach the heart.<br>
<br>
We then asked: Could simply increasing the initial infectious dose allow the otherwise weakly invasive strain CDC7F:2617–97 to cause more severe cardiac damage? To test this, we infected mice with a 100-fold higher dose—10⁵ CFU instead of 10³ CFU—and monitored bacteremia. Surprisingly, even with this much larger starting inoculum, the resulting bloodstream infection was not significantly more severe (*P* = 0.2251). The average blood titer at euthanasia was only 2.48 × 10³ CFU/mL (S2 Fig), which is still relatively low. This suggests that CDC7F:2617–97 may have intrinsic limitations—perhaps due to immune recognition or poor survival in blood—that prevent it from establishing high-grade bacteremia regardless of initial dose.<br>
<br>
However, when we looked at heart invasion, we did observe a modest increase: whereas only 2 out of 6 mice (33.3%) infected with the lower dose (10³ CFU) had detectable bacteria in the heart, 3 out of 5 mice (60%) infected with the higher dose (10⁵ CFU) showed cardiac bacterial presence. So, while more bacteria reached the heart with a higher starting dose, the difference in bacterial burden within the heart tissue itself was not statistically significant (*P* = 0.8701). This hints that increasing the inoculum gives the bacteria a slightly better chance to invade the heart, but it doesn’t guarantee widespread or severe colonization.<br>
<br>
Next, we explored whether the host immune response—specifically neutrophils, which are frontline defenders against bacterial infections—might be limiting CDC7F:2617–97’s ability to cause disease. We pre-treated mice with an antibody against Ly6G, a surface marker on neutrophils, which effectively depletes these cells (and to a lesser extent, monocytes and macrophages [35]). If neutrophils were successfully controlling the infection, removing them should make the disease worse. But in mice challenged with 10³ CFU of CDC7F:2617–97, this depletion did *not* enhance disease severity (*P* = 0.8485). In fact, only 1 out of 5 mice had detectable bacteria in the blood at 26 hours post-infection (at a level of 2.1 × 10⁴ CFU/mL), and none showed elevated troponin-I. This suggests that even without neutrophils, this strain struggles to sustain bacteremia or cause heart damage—further evidence that its attenuation isn’t just due to immune clearance.<br>
<br>
Notably, none of the strains tested—including CDC7F:2617–97—showed susceptibility to complement-mediated killing in vitro after incubation in whole unfixed human serum for two hours (S3 Fig). The complement system is a major arm of innate immunity that can directly punch holes in bacterial membranes. Resistance to complement is typically a hallmark of invasive, virulent pneumococcal strains. The fact that CDC7F:2617–97 survives this assault implies its attenuation must stem from some other defect—perhaps in metabolic fitness, stress resistance, or the ability to adhere to or invade host tissues—rather than vulnerability to immune destruction.<br>
