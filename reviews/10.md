Let’s pause to unpack the logic here, because this is a beautiful example of how the visual system is wired for efficiency. The retina is not a uniform camera sensor; it has specialized regions. The **fovea** (the center of your visual field) is packed with small, densely-packed cells that excel at resolving fine details—these are your *high spatial frequency* detectors. In contrast, the **peripheral retina** (the edges of your vision) is dominated by larger, more sensitive cells that are great at detecting big, coarse shapes—your *low spatial frequency* detectors. This anatomical specialization extends all the way to the visual cortex: the *anterior* striate cortex (the front part of the primary visual area) receives input from the periphery, while the *posterior* striate cortex (the occipital pole, at the very back of the brain) processes foveal information.<br>
<br>
Now, consider what a **checkerboard pattern-reversal stimulus** does. When you flip a large checkerboard (say, with big squares), you are primarily stimulating the peripheral retina and the anterior striate cortex. This is why large checks are so effective at generating a robust **P100m**—the magnetic counterpart of the well-known P100 visual evoked potential (VEP). Conversely, if you use a pattern with very small checks (high spatial frequency), you are preferentially activating the fovea and the occipital pole. This makes small checks better suited for eliciting the *electrical* P100 (measured on the scalp) than the *magnetic* P100m (measured with MEG), because MEG is more sensitive to the tangential sources in the anterior cortex.<br>
<br>
In our study, the magnetic visual evoked field (VEF) response to a reversing checkerboard was essentially a **triphasic wave complex**—a sequence of three distinct peaks. The second, and largest, of these peaks occurred at roughly 100 milliseconds after the stimulus. This pattern is entirely consistent with the well-established **N75m-P100m-N145m** complex (a negative peak at 75 ms, a positive peak at 100 ms, and a negative peak at 145 ms) that has been documented in earlier VEF research. <br>
<br>

<td valign="top"><div>## Visual Evoked Field Responses to Checkerboard Pattern-Reversal: The Role of Check Size and Interstimulus Interval<br>
<br>
When we look at a visual stimulus—like a flickering checkerboard—our brain generates electrical activity that can be measured using techniques such as visual evoked potentials (VEPs) or, in this case, visual evoked fields (VEFs) with magnetoencephalography (MEG). One of the most reliable signals in this response is a large positive wave that peaks around 100 milliseconds after the stimulus appears. In MEG studies, this is called the **P100m**; in EEG, it&#x27;s known as the **P100**. But what determines how strong or fast this signal is? Two key factors explored in this study are the *size* of the checks in the checkerboard and the *timing* between successive stimuli—the interstimulus interval (ISI).<br>
<br>
Let’s start by understanding how **check size** influences brain responses.<br>
<br>
### How Check Size Influences Which Brain Cells Respond<br>
<br>
Imagine the checkerboard you&#x27;re viewing is made up of alternating black and white squares. The size of these squares—called &quot;check size&quot;—determines how detailed the pattern is. Scientists describe this in terms of **spatial frequency**, which is just a fancy way of saying &quot;how many light-dark transitions occur across a given distance in your visual field.&quot;<br>
<br>
- **Large checks** (e.g., 180&#x27; or 450&#x27;) mean **low spatial frequency**: few transitions, broad patterns.<br>
- **Small checks** (e.g., 15&#x27; or 30&#x27;) mean **high spatial frequency**: many transitions, fine details.<br>
<br>
Now here’s the crucial point: your retina isn’t uniform. Different parts of it are specialized for different jobs.<br>
<br>
- The **periphery** of the retina (outside the center) is great at detecting large, coarse patterns—like spotting movement in your side vision.<br>
- The **fovea**, the very center of your retina, is packed with cells that detect fine detail—like reading small text.<br>
<br>
So when you see **large checks**, they mainly activate cells in the **peripheral retina**. These signals travel to the **anterior (front) part of the striate cortex**—the brain’s primary visual area in the occipital lobe. This pathway turns out to be especially effective at generating the **P100m** response.<br>
<br>
On the flip side, **small checks** (high spatial frequency) fall mostly on the **fovea**, activating foveal retinal cells. These connect to the **posterior (back) striate cortex**, near the occipital pole. While this setup is ideal for sharp vision, it&#x27;s *less effective* at producing a strong P100m. Instead, it tends to elicit the classic **P100** seen in EEG studies.<br>
<br>
This distinction helps explain why large checks are more powerful for evoking P100m in MEG experiments—they tap into a neural pathway optimized for coarse, peripheral processing.<br>
<br>
### What Does the VEF Waveform Look Like?<br>
<br>
In this study, the visual evoked field (VEF) response to reversing checkerboards typically showed a **triphasic wave complex**—that is, three main bends in the signal over time. The most prominent of these is the second deflection, which peaks at about **100 milliseconds**. This pattern matches what earlier studies have labeled the **N75m-P100m-N145m complex**, where:<br>
<br>
- **N75m** is a negative wave peaking around 75 ms,<br>
- **P100m** is a large positive wave at ~100 ms,<br>
- **N145m** is a later negative wave at ~145 ms.<br>
<br>
Even though this study didn’t record EEG simultaneously, the researchers reasonably assume that the **P100m** they measured corresponds directly to the **P100** seen in EEG studies. Why? Because the timing and sequence match: a negative wave before (~70 ms), a big positive peak at ~100 ms, and another negative wave after (~150 ms). Previous studies that *did* record both MEG and EEG found **no significant difference in latency** between P100m and P100, suggesting they arise from the same underlying cortical activity—likely in the primary visual cortex.<br>
<br>
Supporting this link, the average P100m latency observed here for **30&#x27; checks** was **115.0 ms** (see Table 1), which closely matches the **118.8 ms** reported in a prior MEG study using **28&#x27; checks** under similar conditions. This consistency across studies strengthens confidence in the reliability of these measurements.<br>
<br>
### Small Checks: Weaker and Slower Responses<br>
<br>
When the researchers used **smaller checks (15&#x27;)**, they observed two clear effects:<br>
<br>
1. **Smaller amplitude** (weaker signal),<br>
2. **Delayed peak latency** (slower response).<br>
<br>
This finding isn’t new—it aligns with earlier VEP studies and a recent MEG study. But *why* does this happen?<br>
<br>
To understand this, we need to think about the **retinal ganglion cells**—the neurons in your retina that send visual information to your brain. These cells aren’t all the same. Those most sensitive to **high spatial frequencies** (fine details) are concentrated in the **fovea**. They have two key features:<br>
<br>
- **Small receptive fields**: they monitor a tiny patch of visual space,<br>
- **Small-diameter axons**: their nerve fibers are thin, which means they conduct signals more slowly.<br>
<br>
Because of this, when you present small checks (high spatial frequency), you&#x27;re primarily activating these **slow-conducting, fovea-centered cells**. That helps explain the **delayed latency** of the P100m response: the signal simply takes longer to reach the brain.<br>
<br>
Moreover, because these cells cover a smaller area and respond less vigorously to large-scale contrast changes, the overall **amplitude** of the brain response is reduced. So, even though small checks provide more detail, they’re actually *less efficient* at driving the kind of widespread neural synchronization needed for a strong P100m—especially when the stimulus is presented in the periphery.<br>
<br>
This fits with the idea of **parallel visual processing**: your brain processes different aspects of vision (like detail vs. motion) through separate but simultaneous pathways. Large checks engage the fast, coarse-processing pathway (via peripheral retina), while small checks rely on the slower, detail-oriented foveal pathway.<br>
<br>
### What Happens with Very Large Checks (&gt;120&#x27;)?<br>
<br>
Interestingly, the effects of **very large checks**—like 180&#x27; or even 450&#x27;—haven’t been widely studied. One prior MEG study by Nakamura and colleagues found that **180&#x27; checks** produced a slightly **longer latency** and **smaller amplitude** compared to **90&#x27; checks**.<br>
<br>
In this study, the results were even more pronounced: P100m responses to **180&#x27; and 450&#x27; checks** showed **progressive weakening** compared to **120&#x27; checks**. That is, bigger wasn’t better—beyond a certain point, increasing check size actually *reduced* the brain’s response.<br>
<br>
Why might this happen? The researchers offer two compelling explanations.<br>
<br>
#### Explanation 1: Too Few Pattern Cycles<br>
<br>
Think of each black-white pair in the checkerboard as one **cycle** of a visual pattern. The number of complete cycles across the visual field matters. In this experiment:<br>
<br>
- **120&#x27; checks** provided about **six full cycles**,<br>
- **180&#x27; checks** gave only **five cycles**,<br>
- **450&#x27; checks** had just **two cycles**.<br>
<br>
Now, here’s a key insight from vision science: the visual system needs a **minimum number of cycles** to reliably detect a pattern. Studies using colored sine-wave gratings (smoothly varying red/green patterns) have shown that **fewer than six cycles** lead to **weaker visual responses**, even if the contrast is high.<br>
<br>
So, the **optimal response at 120&#x27; checks** may not be about size per se, but about hitting that **&quot;sweet spot&quot; of six cycles**—enough for the brain to clearly recognize and respond to the repeating pattern. Larger checks fall short in this regard, offering too few cycles to drive a strong response.<br>
<br>
#### Explanation 2: Mixing of Visual Channels<br>
<br>
There’s another possibility: very large checks might activate different **visual processing channels** in the brain.<br>
<br>
Your visual system has multiple pathways, including:<br>
<br>
- **Luminance channels**: sensitive to brightness differences,<br>
- **Contrast channels**: tuned to edges and pattern changes.<br>
<br>
Typically, pattern-reversal stimuli (like flipping a checkerboard) are designed to isolate **contrast-specific responses**. But with **very large checks**, the brain may start interpreting the stimulus more as a **global brightness change** rather than a detailed pattern. This could **activate luminance-sensitive pathways**, which aren’t as effective at generating the typical P100m.<br>
<br>
In other words, the signal becomes &quot;contaminated&quot; by non-pattern-specific activity, diluting the contrast-driven response. Supporting this idea, earlier studies on retinal responses have shown that **large checks can evoke non-specific electrical activity** in the retina that isn’t tied to pattern processing.<br>
<br>
So, the attenuation of P100m with very large checks might reflect a shift from **pattern-specific** to **luminance-dominated** processing—a kind of neural &quot;mismatch&quot; between what the stimulus intends to probe and what the brain actually responds to.<br>
<br>
### Check Size Also Affects Earlier Components: The Case of N75<br>
<br>
So far, we’ve focused on P100m, but check size doesn’t just affect this one wave. It also influences earlier components like **N75** (or N75m in MEG).<br>
<br>
Multiple VEP studies have shown that as check size increases:<br>
<br>
- **N75 amplitude decreases**,<br>
- **N75 latency shortens**.<br>
<br>
Like P100/P100m, N75m is also modulated by factors like **aging** and **contrast level**, suggesting it reflects meaningful neural processing in the visual cortex.<br>
<br>
Given this, studying how **check size affects N75m** in MEG could provide deeper insight into the early stages of visual processing. However, in this particular study, the researchers chose not to analyze N75m. Why?<br>
<br>
Because when they used a wide range of check sizes, the **N75m response was often too weak or inconsistent to measure reliably** (see Figure 1). Without clear, reproducible signals, drawing firm conclusions would be risky. So while the topic is scientifically interesting, the data limitations prevented further exploration here.<br>
<br>
### Does Timing Between Stimuli Matter? The ISI Effect on P100m<br>
<br>
Finally, what about the **interstimulus interval (ISI)**—the time between one checkerboard reversal and the next?<br>
<br>
This factor has been understudied in MEG. One earlier VEP study found that when ISI increased from **0.25 seconds to 1 second**, the P100 latency **shortened by 4.8 ms**—suggesting that longer breaks between stimuli allow the brain to respond faster.<br>
<br>
But in this study, **no significant change** in P100m activation was observed across different ISIs. That is, whether stimuli came quickly or slowly, the brain’s response (in terms of latency and amplitude) stayed roughly the same.<br>
