Now, a quick word on what &quot;stability&quot; means here. When we say a critical point is a &quot;saddle × center,&quot; we&#x27;re describing what happens to a trajectory that starts very close to that point. The &quot;saddle&quot; part means that in one direction, trajectories are pulled away (unstable), while in the perpendicular direction, they oscillate around the point (the &quot;center&quot; part, which is stable). So, these saddles are like a mountain pass: you can cross them, but you won&#x27;t stay there. The wells, on the other hand, are pure &quot;centers&quot;—they&#x27;re stable in all directions, so trajectories will happily orbit around them.<br>

## 3. The Phase Space Transport Mechanisms Governing Selectivity<br>
<br>
Now we get to the heart of the matter. Our goal is to understand *why* some trajectories visit the wells and others don&#x27;t—and, crucially, to be able to *predict* which will happen for any given trajectory. We&#x27;ll do this by working at a fixed total energy, \( H_0 = 0.1 \). Think of this as setting the &quot;budget&quot; of energy available to our molecule; it can&#x27;t exceed this amount.<br>
<br>
In Fig. 2, we see two qualitatively distinct reactive trajectories, shown in red, projected onto the PES. These are our &quot;representative examples&quot; of the two types of behavior we&#x27;re trying to explain:<br>
<br>
- **A.** The trajectory starts from the entrance region, reaches the energy boundary (the edge of the allowed region) *without* visiting either well, and then returns to the exit region—again, without visiting either well. It&#x27;s like a visitor who walks into a building, glances around the lobby, and walks right back out without checking out any of the rooms.<br>
- **B.** The trajectory starts from the entrance region but *does* visit the region of the top or bottom well. This is the visitor who goes into one of the rooms before leaving.<br>
<br>
In each panel of Fig. 2, we also see three blue curves. These are **unstable periodic orbits (UPOs)**. Let&#x27;s unpack that term. A *periodic orbit* is a trajectory that repeats itself exactly after some time—like a planet orbiting a star. &quot;Unstable&quot; means that if you nudge a trajectory slightly off this orbit, it will drift away over time. These UPOs are the gatekeepers of the system:<br>
<br>
- The UPO of the **upper index-1 saddle** controls entrance to and exit from the reaction region (the whole area we&#x27;re studying).<br>
- The UPO in the region of the **top well** controls entrance and exit to that well.<br>
- The UPO in the region of the **bottom well** controls entrance and exit to that well.<br>
<br>
But how do these UPOs actually *control* anything? The answer lies in their *stable and unstable manifolds*—the sets of all trajectories that approach the UPO in forward time (stable) or backward time (unstable). These manifolds are the &quot;walls&quot; that guide trajectories, and we&#x27;ll see exactly how in a moment.<br>

Now, here&#x27;s the key geometric insight that makes everything click. For a two degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian system (which is what we have here), the energy surface is three-dimensional. The stable and unstable manifolds of a UPO are each *two-dimensional* surfaces within this three-dimensional space. This is a crucial fact: a two-dimensional surface inside a three-dimensional space acts as a *barrier*—it divides the space into separate regions. You can&#x27;t get from one side to the other without crossing the surface.<br>
<br>
And since these manifolds are *invariant* (meaning trajectories that start on them stay on them forever), no trajectory can ever pass *through* them. They are impenetrable walls in phase space. This is the fundamental reason why they constrain the evolution of trajectories: they carve the energy surface into distinct &quot;rooms,&quot; and the only way to move between rooms is through the &quot;doors&quot; created by the intersections of these manifolds.<br>
<br>

<br>

This provides a mechanism for trajectories to leave and return to the neighborhood of a single periodic orbit.<br>
- A **heteroclinic trajectory** is a trajectory that lies in the stable manifold of *one* UPO and the unstable manifold of a *different* UPO. ## 3. The Phase Space Transport Mechanisms Governing Selectivity<br>
<br>
Now we’re going to uncover the hidden machinery behind chemical selectivity—why some trajectories end up in one product well and others in a different one, even when they start out very similarly. This isn’t random; it’s governed by precise structures in phase space. Our goal is to predict the fate of individual trajectories based on the underlying geometry of motion.<br>
<br>
We’ll focus on a fixed total energy: $ H_0 = 0.1 $. Why fix the energy? Because in conservative systems like this one (no friction or external forces), energy is conserved. That means every trajectory must stay on the same “energy surface” in phase space—like hikers who can only walk along a specific elevation contour on a mountain. By fixing $ H_0 $, we’re slicing through the full phase space to look at just the allowed motions at this energy.<br>
<br>
Let’s start by looking at **Figure 2**, where we see two very different kinds of behavior, shown in red. These are actual computed trajectories, projected down onto the 2D potential energy surface (PES)—think of this as watching the shadow of a 4D motion on a 2D map. Even though the full motion happens in position *and* momentum (four variables total), projecting onto $ (x, y) $ helps us visualize what’s happening in real space.<br>
<br>
There are two main types of trajectory behavior we observe:<br>
<br>
**A. Direct reactive trajectories:**  <br>
These start from the entrance channel (near the upper index-1 saddle), cross over the reaction region, and exit—without ever falling into either potential well. They’re like a ball rolling quickly over a hill without getting caught in any side valleys.<br>
<br>
**B. Indirect (or complex-forming) trajectories:**  <br>
These enter the system and get temporarily trapped near one of the two potential wells—either the top or the bottom. They swirl around for a while, as if exploring the neighborhood, before eventually escaping to the exit. This temporary trapping is key to understanding selectivity: depending on which well they visit, they may lead to different chemical outcomes.<br>
<br>
Now, here’s the big question: **What controls whether a trajectory takes path A or B? What decides which well it might visit—or if it gets trapped at all?**<br>
<br>
The answer lies not in the trajectories themselves, but in special invariant structures that act like traffic directors in phase space: **unstable periodic orbits (UPOs)**.<br>
<br>
In each panel of Figure 2, you’ll see three blue curves. These are not random—they are the UPOs of the system:<br>
- The **UPO associated with the upper index-1 saddle** (the red diamond at (0,0))—this one guards the gateway to the entire reaction region.<br>
- The **top UPO**, which lives near the upper potential well.<br>
- The **bottom UPO**, which lives near the lower potential well.<br>
<br>
Each of these UPOs acts like a dynamical checkpoint:<br>
- The **upper UPO** controls access *into and out of* the reaction region. No trajectory can enter or leave the central area without passing near this orbit.<br>
- The **top UPO** regulates traffic going into and out of the top well.<br>
- The **bottom UPO** does the same for the bottom well.<br>
<br>
But how do they “control” anything? After all, they’re just periodic orbits—repeating loops in phase space.<br>
<br>
The magic happens through their **stable and unstable manifolds**—structures that grow out from the UPOs like invisible rails guiding trajectories.<br>
<br>
Let’s build some intuition. Imagine a UPO as a kind of unstable swing that keeps moving in a loop. If you place a ball *exactly* on the swing’s path, it will follow the loop forever. But if you nudge it slightly off, it won’t just wiggle around—it will be *repelled* away along specific directions. These directions define the **unstable manifold**: trajectories that *diverge* from the UPO as time moves forward.<br>
<br>
Conversely, if you could run time backward, you’d see some trajectories that *approach* the UPO—they’re coming in along the **stable manifold**.<br>
<br>
So, the stable manifold is like a set of incoming highways to the UPO’s neighborhood, and the unstable manifold is a set of outgoing highways.<br>
<br>
In a two-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian system (like ours), each UPO lives in a 3D energy surface (because energy is fixed), and its stable and unstable manifolds are **2D surfaces** within that 3D space.<br>
<br>
Now here’s the crucial point: **2D surfaces in a 3D space can act as barriers.** Just like a piece of paper floating in a room divides the room into two sides, these invariant manifolds divide the energy surface into regions. And because they’re *invariant*—meaning trajectories can’t cross them—they act as one-way membranes that guide and constrain motion.<br>
<br>
But they don’t just sit there passively. They twist and fold, and sometimes, the stable and unstable manifolds of a UPO **intersect** each other. When that happens, something profound occurs.<br>
<br>
Let’s define two key types of intersections:<br>
<br>
- A **homoclinic trajectory** is one that lies in *both* the stable and unstable manifold of the *same* UPO. It leaves the neighborhood of the orbit, wanders far away, and then comes back to it—like a boomerang trajectory.<br>
  <br>
- A **heteroclinic trajectory** lies in the stable manifold of one UPO and the unstable manifold of *another*. It’s a bridge: it leaves the neighborhood of one orbit and eventually approaches another.<br>
<br>
Now, here’s why these matter: **the existence of homoclinic or heteroclinic connections implies that the manifolds intersect not just at a point, but along entire curves.** And because the manifolds are 2D, their intersections can enclose 2D regions called **lobes**.<br>
<br>
These lobes are like transport capsules. As the manifolds evolve in time, these lobes move, stretch, and fold—sweeping through phase space and carrying trajectories from one region to another. They control how trajectories get transferred between the entrance, the wells, and the exit.<br>
<br>
For example, a lobe attached to the upper UPO’s unstable manifold might sweep into the region near the top well, capturing trajectories and funneling them into that well. Another lobe might direct flow toward the bottom well. The timing and geometry of these lobe dynamics determine which products are formed—this is the essence of **phase space transport governing selectivity**.<br>
<br>
So, when you look at the red trajectories in Figure 2, don’t just see lines on a plot. See them as passengers being carried by an invisible network of highways (the manifolds) and shuttled by moving lobes—guided by the architecture of UPOs and their connections.<br>
<br>
This framework turns a seemingly chaotic set of paths into a predictable, geometric story. And once we understand this structure, we can answer questions like:  <br>
- Which initial conditions lead to which product?  <br>
- How does changing energy affect the branching ratio?  <br>
- Can we design controls to steer trajectories toward a desired outcome?<br>
<br>
All of this hinges on the intricate web formed by stable and unstable manifolds—and the homoclinic and heteroclinic tangles they create.<br>
