Before diving into strength training, participants began with lighter weights—well below what they might eventually lift—to focus purely on mastering the correct movement patterns. This is a smart and safe strategy: you wouldn’t try to run a marathon before learning to walk, and the same logic applies here. By starting light, the body learns the proper mechanics of each exercise, reducing the risk of injury and building a strong foundation for future progress.<br>
<br>
Each exercise session consisted of **three sets of 12 repetitions**. Between sets and between different exercises, participants took a recovery break of about **90 seconds**. This rest period is carefully chosen—it’s long enough to allow some recovery of energy stores in the muscles, but short enough to maintain an effective training stimulus and keep the session efficient.<br>
<br>
Now, let’s talk about how researchers determined each participant’s **one-repetition maximum**, or **1RM**—the heaviest weight a person can lift just once with proper form. This is a gold standard in strength assessment because it gives a clear, measurable benchmark of muscular strength. To find this value, testers typically needed between **four and six attempts**. But they didn’t just jump in with heavy weights—that would be dangerous. Instead, they followed a careful, step-by-step process.<br>
<br>
First, the participant warmed up by performing **6 to 10 repetitions** using about **50% of their estimated maximum load**. Think of this like revving a car engine gently before driving fast—it prepares the muscles, tendons, and nervous system for heavier work. After this warm-up, they rested for **two minutes**, giving their body time to reset before the first real attempt.<br>
<br>
Then came the test itself. The participant tried to complete **two full repetitions** with a challenging (but not maximal) weight. Why two? Because it’s a practical way to estimate whether the load is close to their true 1RM without pushing to absolute failure on the first try.<br>
<br>
Here’s where the smart, adaptive testing protocol kicks in:<br>
<br>
- If the participant **successfully completed two reps**, the researchers knew the weight was too light. So, in the next attempt, they **increased the load**.<br>
- If the participant **failed to complete even one repetition**, the weight was too heavy, so they **decreased it** in the next try.<br>
<br>
Between these attempts, participants rested for **3 to 5 minutes**—a longer break than between regular sets. This ensures that fatigue doesn’t unfairly influence performance, so the test measures true strength, not just tiredness.<br>
<br>
If the 1RM wasn’t pinpointed after the second attempt, a **third and final attempt** was made, again adjusting the weight up or down based on performance, with a **five-minute rest** between exercises. This systematic, feedback-driven approach allows for a precise and safe estimation of maximum strength.<br>
<br>
All testing was closely supervised, and every participant received the **same level of encouragement**. This is crucial in research—it keeps the conditions fair and consistent across all individuals, so differences in performance reflect real physiological differences, not variations in motivation or coaching.<br>
<br>
The order of the strength tests mattered too. Participants were tested first on the **leg extension machine** (which works the quadriceps, a large muscle group), and then on the **barbell biceps curl** (which targets the smaller biceps). Why this order? Because larger muscle groups tend to require more energy and focus, and testing them first ensures they’re assessed when the body is freshest. It also reduces the chance that fatigue from arm exercises could indirectly affect leg performance through overall exhaustion.<br>
<br>
To ensure consistency and fairness, the **technique for each exercise was standardized** using guidelines from Faigenbaum and Westcott—experts in youth resistance training. This means every participant performed the movements the same way, making the results more reliable and comparable.<br>
<br>
A repetition was considered **failed** if the participant couldn’t complete the full range of motion in **both** of the two attempted reps. This strict criterion ensures that strength is measured with proper form—no shortcuts or partial movements that could inflate results artificially.<br>

**Idade cronológica e maturação sexual**  <br>
*(Chronological Age and Sexual Maturation)*<br>
<br>
Chronological age was expressed in **decimal (milesimal) form**—meaning, for example, a child who is 12 years and 6 months old would be recorded as 12.5 years. This method, following Healy et al., allows for more precise statistical analysis than just using whole years.<br>
<br>
But chronological age doesn’t tell the whole story—especially in young people. Two 13-year-olds can be at very different stages of physical development. That’s why researchers also assessed **biological maturation**, which reflects how far along a person is in puberty.<br>
<br>
This was done using the **Tanner staging system**, a well-established method that classifies development based on secondary sexual characteristics. Participants self-assessed their **genital development** and **pubic hair growth** by comparing themselves to standardized photographic charts developed by Tanner and later adapted by Matsudo for the population in this study. Additionally, researchers visually assessed **underarm (axillary) hair** development.<br>
<br>
These markers give a much clearer picture of a participant’s true biological stage—not just how old they are on paper, but how mature their body is. This is especially important in studies involving strength and physical performance, as a biologically more mature teenager can have significant advantages in muscle mass and power, regardless of their chronological age.<br>

**Análise estatística**  <br>
*(Statistical Analysis)*<br>
<br>
Before running any tests, researchers checked whether the data followed a **normal distribution**—a bell-shaped curve where most values cluster around the average. They used the **Shapiro-Wilk test** for this, which is particularly reliable for smaller sample sizes. If the p-value was **≤ 0.05**, the data were considered *not* normally distributed, meaning they had to use statistical methods that don’t assume normality.<br>
<br>
To describe the sample, they used **descriptive statistics**: mean (average), standard deviation (how spread out the data are), median (middle value), and the minimum and maximum values. These give a clear snapshot of who was in the study and how they varied.<br>
<br>
To explore relationships between variables—like whether greater muscle strength correlates with more advanced maturation—they used the **Spearman correlation coefficient**. This is a non-parametric test, meaning it works well with data that aren’t normally distributed. It measures the strength and direction of a monotonic relationship (whether one variable tends to increase as the other increases, even if not in a straight line).<br>
<br>
Statistical significance was set at **p &lt; 0.01** and **p ≤ 0.05**—in other words, a finding was considered meaningful if there was less than a 1% or 5% chance it occurred by random chance.<br>
<br>
All analyses were performed using **SPSS 13.0**, a widely used software package for statistical analysis in the social and health sciences.<br>
