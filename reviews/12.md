<br>
Before we can begin exploring the data, we need to prepare it for analysis. This is a crucial first step in any scientific investigation, as the choices we make here will influence everything that follows.<br>
<br>
**Standardizing the Measurement:**<br>
First, we need to ensure that all IgE measurements are on the same scale. The original data were reported in nanograms per milliliter (ng/ml), but the scientific community often uses international units (IU). To make our results comparable with other studies, we convert all values using the conversion factor **1 IU = 2.4 ng/ml**. This is a simple unit conversion, like converting inches to centimeters, but it is essential for consistency.<br>
<br>
**The Log Transformation:** Next, we encounter a fundamental property of IgE levels: they are **log-normally distributed**. This means that if you were to plot the raw IgE values, the distribution would be heavily skewed to the right—most people have low values, but a few have extremely high values. This skewness can cause problems for many statistical tests, which assume a normal (bell-shaped) distribution. To address this, we apply a **natural log transformation** (lnIgE) to all values before any analysis. This transformation compresses the high values and stretches the low values, resulting in a distribution that is approximately normal. Think of it like using a logarithmic scale on a graph to see both small and large values clearly. This is a standard practice in immunology and biostatistics.<br>
<br>
**Interpreting the Means:** When we report means from t-tests, we will report **geometric means**. This is a direct consequence of the log transformation. The geometric mean is calculated by taking the exponential of the mean of the log-transformed values. In practical terms, the geometric mean represents the &quot;typical&quot; value in a log-normal distribution better than the arithmetic mean, which would be inflated by the few extremely high values.<br>
<br>
**Software Used:** The descriptive statistics and t-tests were performed using **PASW Statistics 18.0** (formerly known as SPSS). All other, more complex analyses were conducted in **R 2.10.1**, a powerful and free statistical computing environment.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
**Modeling the Age Pattern of IgE**<br>
<br>
Our primary goal is to understand how IgE levels change with age in each of our three populations. However, we don&#x27;t want to assume a specific shape for this relationship (like a straight line). Instead, we want to let the data &quot;speak&quot; and reveal the pattern. This is where **Generalized Additive Models (GAMs)** come in. GAMs are a flexible extension of linear regression that allow us to model non-linear relationships. Instead of fitting a single line, a GAM fits a smooth curve through the data points. This curve is constructed using **thin plate regression splines**, which are essentially a series of connected polynomial segments that are smoothed together.<br>
<br>
**Setting Up the Model:** To fit a GAM, we need to specify a few things. First, we need to define the **basis knots**. These are the points where the spline segments are joined. Since the number of cases at each age is not uniform across the populations, we specify the knots based on the **even ten-percentiles of the age distribution**. This means we divide the age range into ten intervals, each containing an equal number of data points. This ensures that the model has enough data to estimate the curve in each segment, even if the ages are not evenly spaced. The vertical lines in Figure 2A show these knot locations. The smoothing parameters, which control how wiggly the curve is, are generated automatically by the software. Each model includes an intercept, a factor for sex, a spline for age, and a spline for the interaction between age and sex. This allows the shape of the age curve to be different for males and females.<br>
<br>
**A Problem with the Tsimane Data:** The initial models revealed a problem. The Shuar and NHANES models predicted similar IgE levels at birth (around 7-21 IU/ml). However, the Tsimane model predicted peak IgE at birth, which is biologically implausible. This is because there are very few Tsimane children under the age of five in the dataset, so the model has little data to work with and essentially draws a straight line that peaks at the earliest age. This is a classic problem with sparse data at the extremes.<br>
<br>
**Anchoring the Tsimane Model:** To correct this, we turned to the scientific literature. Numerous studies have found that IgE levels at birth are extremely low (less than 1 IU/ml), even in infants of mothers with high IgE due to helminth infections. Given this strong prior evidence, and the convergence of the other two models, we decided to **anchor** the Tsimane model to a biologically plausible intercept at birth. We did this by adding **dummy cases** to the dataset: artificial data points with age zero and an IgE value of 15 IU/ml. These dummy cases were included in the GAM model to pull the curve down to a realistic starting point, but they were excluded from all other statistical analyses. This is a transparent and justifiable way to incorporate prior knowledge into the model.<br>
<br>
**Estimating Odds-Ratios:** In addition to modeling IgE levels, we also used GAMs with a **binomial logit-link function** to estimate the odds-ratios for Tsimane helminth infection by age. This is a different type of GAM used for binary outcomes (infected vs. not infected). We also estimated the association between helminth infection and IgE levels using linear models, controlling for infection with other helminths, sex, and age.<br>
<br>
**Verifying the Age Shapes:** To ensure our results are robust, we used two additional methods to verify the age shapes and compare populations. The first method is a **stepwise linear regression**. This approach identifies critical age-related changes in lnIgE by creating dummy variables for each unique age. Each dummy variable indicates whether a case is older than that specific age. <td valign="top"><div>## Data Analysis<br>
<br>
Before we begin analyzing the data, it’s important to understand how the key variable—**IgE** (Immunoglobulin E)—was handled. IgE is a type of antibody involved in allergic responses and defense against parasites. In this study, IgE levels were originally measured in nanograms per milliliter (ng/ml), but to make comparisons easier across studies and populations, they were converted into **international units (IU)** using a standard conversion:  <br>
&gt; **1 IU = 2.4 ng/ml**<br>
<br>
This means if someone had an IgE level of 2.4 ng/ml, that would be exactly 1 IU/ml. This conversion ensures consistency with other scientific literature.<br>
<br>
Now, here’s a crucial point: IgE levels in human populations don’t follow a normal “bell curve” distribution. Instead, they are **log-normally distributed**—meaning most people have relatively low IgE levels, but a few individuals have very high values, which pulls the distribution to the right (it’s skewed). You can see this clearly in Figure 1, where the data for all three populations—Tsimane, Shuar, and NHANES—show this characteristic skew.<br>
<br>
👉 *Why does this matter?*  <br>
Because many common statistical tests (like t-tests) assume that the data are normally distributed. If we use them on raw IgE values, we risk getting misleading results. To fix this, we apply a **natural logarithm transformation** (written as **lnIgE**) to the data. This transformation &quot;pulls in&quot; the extreme high values and makes the distribution much closer to normal, satisfying the assumptions of our statistical tools.<br>
<br>
So from this point forward, unless otherwise noted, all analyses use **lnIgE**, the log-transformed version of IgE.<br>
<br>
When reporting results like means from these transformed data, we can’t just report the average of the lnIgE values and call it a day—that wouldn’t make intuitive sense to readers who think in terms of actual IgE concentrations. Instead, we calculate **geometric means**. Here&#x27;s how:<br>
<br>
- We take the mean of the **lnIgE** values (which is valid statistically),<br>
- Then we **exponentiate** that mean (i.e., compute $ e^{\text{mean of lnIgE}} $) to convert it back to the original scale.<br>
<br>
This gives us the **geometric mean**, which represents a kind of &quot;average&quot; IgE level that respects the skewed nature of the data. For example, if the mean lnIgE is 3, then the geometric mean IgE is $ e^3 \approx 20 $ IU/ml. This is different from the arithmetic mean, which would be inflated by outliers.<br>
<br>
All descriptive statistics and t-tests were performed using **PASW Statistics 18.0** (formerly known as SPSS), a widely used software package for social sciences and biomedical research. All other analyses—including more advanced modeling—were conducted in **R 2.10.1**, a powerful open-source programming environment for statistical computing (available at www.r-project.org).<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
To explore how IgE levels change with age in each population, the researchers used **Generalized Additive Models (GAMs)** [43,44]. Let’s unpack what that means.<br>
<br>
Traditional linear models assume relationships are straight lines: for example, “IgE increases by X units per year.” But biology is rarely that simple. The relationship between IgE and age might rise quickly in early childhood, plateau in adulthood, or even decline later in life. GAMs are perfect for this kind of situation because they allow us to model **non-linear, smooth patterns** without forcing the data into a rigid shape.<br>
<br>
Think of it like drawing a freehand curve through a scatterplot that captures the overall trend—except the computer does it mathematically, choosing the best-fitting smooth line based on the data.<br>
<br>
In this study, the GAMs were fitted using the `gam` function in R’s **mgcv** package, which is one of the most popular tools for this type of analysis. The models used **thin plate regression splines** [45,46]—a fancy name for a flexible mathematical method that creates smooth curves by piecing together polynomial functions, minimizing roughness while staying close to the data.<br>
<br>
But here’s a challenge: the number of participants in each population wasn’t evenly spread across ages. For instance, there might be many adults but very few young children in one group. If we placed model &quot;knots&quot; (points where the curve can change direction) at equal age intervals, some regions would have too little data to estimate the curve reliably.<br>
<br>
To solve this, the researchers set the initial **basis knots** based on **even percentiles of the age distribution**—specifically, every 10%. That means there’s roughly the same number of people between each pair of knots. This ensures that each segment of the curve is supported by a similar amount of data, improving the reliability of the fit. You can visualize this setup in Figure 2.<br>
<br>
Once the knot locations were set, the model automatically determined the **smoothing parameters**—essentially, how wiggly or smooth the final curve should be—using the default settings in the `gam` function [45]. This prevents overfitting (where the curve follows noise instead of the true pattern).<br>
<br>
Each GAM included several components:<br>
- An **intercept** (the baseline level),<br>
- A **sex factor** (to account for differences between males and females),<br>
- A **spline for age** (to capture the overall non-linear trend),<br>
- And a **spline for the age-by-sex interaction** (to allow the pattern of change with age to differ between males and females).<br>
<br>
This last term is especially important—it lets the model ask: *Does IgE rise faster in boys than in girls? Do women’s levels peak earlier than men’s?* Without this interaction, we’d assume both sexes follow the same age trajectory, which might not be true.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
When the researchers first ran these models, here’s what they found:<br>
<br>
For the **Shuar** and **NHANES** populations, the models predicted similar IgE levels at birth:<br>
- **Shuar**: ~7 IU/ml for females, ~9 IU/ml for males<br>
- **NHANES**: ~15 IU/ml for females, ~21 IU/ml for males<br>
<br>
These values aren’t identical, but they show a consistent pattern: males start slightly higher than females, and both groups have measurable IgE at birth.<br>
<br>
However, the **Tsimane** model behaved differently. Because there were relatively few Tsimane children under five years old, the model didn’t have enough data to detect any curvature in the early years. As a result, the initial fit was essentially a straight line—with the **highest IgE level predicted at birth**, and then a steady decline with age.<br>
<br>
But wait—does that make biological sense?<br>
<br>
Multiple previous studies have shown that **IgE levels at birth are extremely low—typically less than 1 IU/ml** [47–52], even in babies born to mothers with high IgE due to parasitic infections [53,54]. Newborns haven’t yet been exposed to most environmental triggers or parasites, so their immune systems haven’t started producing much IgE.<br>
<br>
Given this strong biological expectation—and the fact that both the Shuar and NHANES models showed low but detectable levels at birth—the researchers decided to **anchor the Tsimane model to a more realistic starting point**.<br>
<br>
They did this by adding **dummy cases**—artificial data points—with the following properties:<br>
- Age = 0 (birth)<br>
- IgE = 15 IU/ml (converted to lnIgE for analysis)<br>
<br>
These dummy cases were included **only in the GAM models**, not in any other statistical tests. Their purpose was to &quot;pull&quot; the beginning of the curve down toward a biologically plausible value, helping the spline fit a more realistic shape despite the lack of real infant data.<br>
<br>
It’s like putting a pin at birth to hold the curve in place while letting the rest of it flex according to the real data. This technique is a common workaround when data are sparse in critical regions, as long as the anchoring is justified by external evidence—which it clearly is here.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
Beyond GAMs, the researchers also used a **binomial logit-link GAM** to estimate the **odds ratios of helminth infection by age** in the Tsimane population. This model helps answer: *How does the probability of having a parasitic worm infection change as people get older?* By using a logit link, the model ensures predictions stay within valid probability bounds (between 0 and 1), while still allowing for smooth, non-linear age trends.<br>
<br>
Additionally, to examine how **helminth infections relate to IgE levels**, the team used **linear models** (on lnIgE) that controlled for:<br>
- Infection with other types of helminths (to isolate effects),<br>
- Sex,<br>
- And age.<br>
<br>
This way, they could ask: *Even after accounting for age and sex, do people with worms tend to have higher IgE?*<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
To double-check the age-related patterns seen in the GAMs and to compare populations more directly, two additional analytical approaches were used.<br>
<br>
The first was a **stepwise linear regression with dummy variables** designed to detect sharp changes in IgE levels at specific ages. Here’s how it worked:<br>
<br>
For each unique age in the dataset (e.g., 1, 2, 3... years), a **dummy variable** was created that indicated whether a person was **older than that age**. For example, the dummy for age 5 would be 0 for everyone 5 or younger, and 1 for everyone older than 5.<br>
