The 24-hour urine collection showed no elevation of metanephrines, which are breakdown products of catecholamines (hormones like adrenaline). This is an important negative result: it tells us that the patient does not have a tumor that secretes these hormones, which is a key feature of pheochromocytoma, a tumor commonly associated with MEN2A. Similarly, the serum intact parathormone level was normal, ruling out hyperparathyroidism. So, despite carrying a known MEN2A-causing mutation, the patient has not developed any of the classic MEN2A-associated tumors (medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, or parathyroid adenoma) during the 6-year follow-up period. This is clinically reassuring but also raises an important question: why is this patient protected, or is it just a matter of time?<br>
<br>
Because MEN2A is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, meaning that a single copy of the mutated *RET* gene is enough to cause the syndrome, it is standard practice to offer genetic counseling and testing to all first-degree relatives (parents, siblings, and children) of an affected individual (Wells et al., 2015). When the family history was taken in detail, a striking pattern emerged: the patient’s mother and her aunt both had breast cancer, and both tragically died from brain metastases. This is a classic red flag for hereditary breast cancer syndrome, which is also autosomal dominant. Unfortunately, no tissue samples from these two deceased relatives were available for *BRCA1/2* testing, so we cannot confirm their genetic status directly. However, the clinical history is so suggestive that the clinical team recommended *BRCA1/2* analysis for the proband (the patient we are following) (Moyer, 2014).<br>
<br>
The result was indeed positive: the proband carries a pathogenic, heterozygous frameshift mutation in the *BRCA1* gene, designated *BRCA1* Ile90Serfs (NC_000017.10:g.41256905_41256917 delTGAAAAGCACAAA). Let’s unpack this notation. &quot;Heterozygous&quot; means that only one of the two copies of the *BRCA1* gene is mutated; the other copy is normal. &quot;Frameshift&quot; means that a small deletion (in this case, 13 base pairs) shifts the reading frame of the gene, completely scrambling the protein sequence downstream of the mutation, which almost always leads to a non-functional protein. This is a classic, high-risk mutation for breast and ovarian cancer. However, a clinical breast exam and a breast MRI showed no signs of malignancy, and 6 years later, the patient still has no evidence of breast or ovarian cancer. This is a good example of the difference between having a genetic predisposition and actually developing the disease—the mutation increases risk dramatically, but it does not guarantee that cancer will occur.<br>
<br>
Given these striking results, the family was brought back for a second round of genetic counseling. After obtaining informed consent, they were tested for both the *RET* exon 11 mutation (the specific mutation found in the proband) and the *BRCA1* mutation. The proband’s brother (age 36) and his two sons (ages 4 and 6) all carried the *RET* p.C634W mutation, but none of them had the *BRCA1* mutation. This is a classic example of independent segregation of two different genetic traits. In the brother, two thyroid masses were found, and his serum calcitonin level was elevated at 215 pg/ml (normal is typically &lt;10 pg/ml). Calcitonin is a hormone produced by the parafollicular C-cells of the thyroid, and a high level is a sensitive marker for medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC). A total thyroidectomy was performed, and his calcitonin levels returned to normal, indicating that the tumor was successfully removed. The brother’s two sons, who were only 4 and 6 years old, underwent prophylactic thyroidectomy—a preventive surgery to remove the thyroid before cancer can develop. The pathology on their removed thyroids showed no signs of MTC, which is the goal of early intervention. The proband’s father, however, carried neither the *RET* nor the *BRCA1* mutation. **DISCUSSION**<br>
<br>
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported patient carrying both a pathogenic *RET* and a pathogenic *BRCA1* germline mutation. The combination of two hereditary cancer syndromes is extraordinarily rare and presents a unique challenge for clinical management, because the patient must be screened for two completely different sets of cancers, each with its own surveillance protocol. Let’s put the rarity in perspective. The prevalence of MEN2 syndrome is approximately 1 in 30,000 (Kloos et al., 2009), while the prevalence of *BRCA1*2 mutations in the general population is estimated at between 1 in 330 and 1 in 500 (Moyer, 2014). If these two events were independent, the probability of a single person having both would be roughly 1 in 10 to 15 million. This is an extremely rare event, and it is consistent with data from a database that collects cases where two pathogenic mutations in hereditary cancer genes are identified—only 39 such cases have been uploaded (Stradella et al., 2018). This rarity is also a reminder that when we see a patient with a known hereditary syndrome, we should not assume that they cannot also carry a second, unrelated mutation.<br>
<br>
It is important to note that standard next-generation sequencing (NGS) cancer gene panels often do not include the *RET* or *MEN1* genes, which are associated with MEN2 and MEN1, respectively. This is a critical gap. For example, a study by Shin et al. used a panel of 64 genes to screen 252 breast cancer patients. They found that 67 patients (26.8%) carried a total of 77 pathogenic mutations, including 12 in *BRCA1*, 13 in *BRCA2*, and, notably, 7 in *RET*. However, all seven *RET* mutations were found in patients who were *BRCA*-negative (Shin et al., 2017). This suggests that *RET* mutations may be more common in breast cancer patients than previously thought, but because *RET* is not routinely included in breast cancer panels, these mutations are often missed. This is a critical point for clinical practice: if a patient has a strong family history of cancer but tests negative for the common genes, we should consider expanding the panel to include genes like *RET*.<br>

Measurement of metanephrines in the 24-hour urine showed no evidence of a tumor secreting catecholamines or their metabolites—substances often overproduced in pheochromocytomas, a type of adrenal gland tumor. This is an important test because one of the hallmark features of Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2A (MEN2A) is the development of pheochromocytoma. Since the levels were normal, there was no biochemical sign pointing to this condition. Additionally, the patient’s serum intact parathormone (also known as parathyroid hormone, or PTH) was within the normal range. This hormone is produced by the parathyroid glands, and abnormal levels can signal hyperparathyroidism—a second key component of MEN2A. With both tests coming back normal, and no clinical signs of either pheochromocytoma or parathyroid disease, the picture was incomplete despite the presence of a known disease-causing mutation.<br>
<br>
Over the next 6 years of follow-up, the patient (referred to as the proband—the first individual in a family identified with a condition) remained free of any tumors typically associated with MEN2A. This is noteworthy because individuals with pathogenic *RET* mutations, especially the specific one this patient carries (more on that soon), usually develop medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) at a young age, often requiring preventive surgery. The absence of disease manifestations in this case raised questions: Was the *RET* mutation truly pathogenic in her? Or was something else modifying its effects?<br>
<br>
Now, let’s step back and consider the bigger picture. MEN2A is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. What does that mean? It means that only one copy of the mutated gene (in this case, *RET*) is enough to significantly increase the risk of developing the syndrome. Each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of inheriting the mutation. Because of this inheritance pattern, clinical guidelines strongly recommend genetic counseling and testing for first-degree relatives—parents, siblings, and children—of any individual found to carry a pathogenic *RET* mutation (Wells et al., 2015). The goal is early detection and, when appropriate, preventive interventions like thyroidectomy.<br>
<br>
When the medical team dug deeper into the family history, they uncovered a striking pattern: the patient’s mother and her maternal aunt had both developed breast cancer and ultimately died from brain metastases. This is not a typical feature of MEN2A. Instead, this kind of clustering—especially with early-onset breast cancer and fatal metastatic disease—raises a red flag for hereditary breast cancer syndromes, such as those caused by mutations in the *BRCA1* or *BRCA2* genes.<br>
<br>
Here’s why this matters: *BRCA1* and *BRCA2* are tumor suppressor genes. Normally, they help repair damaged DNA and prevent cells from growing and dividing too rapidly. When a person inherits a harmful mutation in one of these genes, their ability to repair DNA is compromised, leading to a much higher lifetime risk of breast, ovarian, and certain other cancers. Like MEN2A, hereditary breast cancer due to *BRCA1/2* mutations follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. That means if one parent carries the mutation, each child has a 50% chance of inheriting it.<br>
<br>
Unfortunately, no biological samples (like blood or tissue) were available from the mother or aunt to test for *BRCA1/2* mutations directly. But because the pattern strongly suggested a hereditary cause, and because the patient herself was at risk, it made perfect sense to test her for these mutations—even though her initial presentation was focused on MEN2A.<br>
<br>
And here’s where things got really interesting: genetic testing revealed that the patient carried a pathogenic, heterozygous frameshift mutation in the *BRCA1* gene. Let’s unpack that.<br>
<br>
- **Pathogenic** means the mutation is known to cause disease.<br>
- **Heterozygous** means she has one normal copy of the gene and one mutated copy—typical in inherited cancer syndromes.<br>
- A **frameshift mutation** is a type of genetic error that occurs when a small number of DNA building blocks (nucleotides) are inserted or deleted, throwing off the entire reading frame of the gene. It’s like removing one or two letters from a sentence written without spaces: “thecatruns” becomes “thc atr uns”—nonsensical. In genes, this usually leads to a truncated, nonfunctional protein.<br>
<br>
The specific mutation was named **BRCA1 Ile90Serfs**, and its genomic location is given as **NC_000017.10:g.41256905_41256917 delTGAAAAGCACAAA**. Let’s break this down:<br>
<br>
- **NC_000017.10** is the reference sequence for chromosome 17.<br>
- **g.41256905_41256917** tells us the exact position on the chromosome where the deletion occurs.<br>
- **delTGAAAAGCACAAA** means that the DNA sequence “TGAAAAGCACAAA” (13 nucleotides) is deleted.<br>
<br>
This deletion shifts the reading frame early in the gene (at amino acid 90), leading to a premature stop signal and a severely shortened BRCA1 protein that can’t perform its DNA repair function. This mutation is well-documented as disease-causing.<br>
<br>
Despite carrying this high-risk *BRCA1* mutation, the patient herself showed no signs of breast or ovarian cancer. Clinical examination and breast MRI were normal at diagnosis, and even 6 years later, she remained cancer-free. This doesn’t mean she’s out of danger—her lifetime risk of breast cancer is still very high (up to 70%)—but it does show that cancer development is not immediate and can be influenced by other genetic or environmental factors.<br>
<br>
These findings completely changed the clinical picture. Now, instead of just managing a potential MEN2A case, the team was dealing with *two* inherited cancer syndromes in one person: MEN2A (from the *RET* mutation) and hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome (from the *BRCA1* mutation). This dual diagnosis has major implications for the entire family.<br>
<br>
So, the patient’s relatives were brought back for **genetic counseling**—a critical step that helps people understand their genetic risks, the meaning of test results, and their options for screening or prevention. After informed consent, family members were tested for both the *RET* exon 11 mutation (the specific *RET* change linked to MEN2A) and the *BRCA1* frameshift mutation.<br>
<br>
Here’s what they found:<br>
<br>
- The **proband’s brother** (age 36) carried the *RET* p.C634W mutation but *not* the *BRCA1* mutation. The p.C634W mutation is a well-known, high-risk variant in the *RET* gene, strongly associated with early-onset medullary thyroid cancer. Sure enough, two thyroid masses were found in him, and his serum calcitonin—a biomarker for MTC—was elevated at 215 pg/ml (normal is typically &lt;10 pg/ml in non-smokers). He underwent total thyroidectomy (complete removal of the thyroid gland), and afterward, his calcitonin levels returned to normal, indicating successful removal of the tumor tissue.<br>
<br>
- His **two sons** (ages 4 and 6) also inherited the *RET* p.C634W mutation. Because of the high risk and early onset of MTC in *RET* mutation carriers, **prophylactic thyroidectomy**—surgery to remove the thyroid *before* cancer develops—is the standard of care. Both boys underwent the procedure. Importantly, histopathological examination of the removed thyroid tissue showed no signs of medullary thyroid cancer. This is a success story: intervention *before* cancer developed, thanks to genetic testing.<br>
<br>
- The **proband’s father** was tested and found to carry *neither* the *RET* mutation nor the *BRCA1* mutation. This is a crucial piece of information for understanding how these mutations were inherited.<br>

**DISCUSSION**<br>
<br>
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a single individual carrying both a pathogenic germline mutation in *RET* (causing MEN2A) and a pathogenic germline mutation in *BRCA1* (causing hereditary breast and ovarian cancer). Germline mutations are those present in every cell of the body, inherited from a parent, and can be passed on to offspring. Somatic mutations, in contrast, occur later in life and are not inherited.<br>
<br>
The co-occurrence of two distinct hereditary cancer syndromes in one person is extremely rare. Why? Let’s think about the odds.<br>
<br>
MEN2 syndrome has an estimated prevalence of about **1 in 30,000** people (Kloos et al., 2009). That means, on average, only about 3 out of every 100,000 individuals carry a disease-causing *RET* mutation. On the other hand, *BRCA1/2* mutations are more common in the general population, with an estimated prevalence between **1 in 330 and 1 in 500** (Moyer, 2014). If we assume these two conditions are independent (i.e., one doesn’t influence the likelihood of the other), we can estimate the probability of someone having both by multiplying the two frequencies:<br>
<br>
- Using the lower bound: (1/30,000) × (1/500) = 1/15,000,000<br>
- Using the higher bound: (1/30,000) × (1/330) ≈ 1/9,900,000<br>
<br>
So, we’re looking at a rough estimate of **1 in 10 to 15 million** people who might carry both mutations. That’s rarer than winning a major lottery—and explains why such cases are almost never seen in clinical practice.<br>
<br>
This theoretical estimate aligns with real-world data. A database compiling cases of individuals with two pathogenic mutations in cancer susceptibility genes has recorded only **39 such cases** to date (Stradella et al., 2018). This scarcity underscores how unusual dual diagnoses are.<br>
<br>
It’s also important to note a limitation in current genetic testing practices: many next-generation sequencing (NGS) cancer gene panels—especially those used in breast cancer clinics—do *not* include *RET* or *MEN1* genes. These panels are often designed to detect mutations commonly linked to breast and ovarian cancer, like *BRCA1/2*, *PALB2*, or *TP53*, but may miss genes associated with endocrine tumors.<br>
<br>
For example, a study by Shin et al. (2017) performed panel sequencing of 64 genes in 252 breast cancer patients. They found that 67 patients (26.8%) carried one or more pathogenic mutations. Among these, 7 patients had mutations in the *RET* gene. But here’s the key point: **all seven of these *RET* mutations were found in patients who were *negative* for *BRCA1/2* mutations**. None of the *BRCA*-positive patients had a *RET* mutation in this cohort.<br>
<br>
This suggests that even when both genes are analyzed, the co-occurrence is exceptionally rare. It also highlights a potential blind spot: if a breast cancer patient with a family history of thyroid cancer is only tested for *BRCA1/2*, a *RET* mutation might be missed—unless a broader panel is used.<br>
<br>
Now, returning to this family: the pedigree and genetic analysis revealed that the proband’s father does *not* carry the *RET* mutation. This is a critical clue. Since the *RET* mutation is autosomal dominant, and the father lacks it, that means the proband must have inherited the *RET* mutation from her *mother*—the same mother who had breast cancer and died of brain metastases.<br>
