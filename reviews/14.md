<td valign="top"><div>## 5.1 Simulation Studies<br>
<br>
To compare the global performance of the different estimators we consider, we need a way to measure how close an estimated probability distribution is to the true underlying distribution. For each set of parameters $\theta = (\mu, \sigma, \lambda)$, we use a powerful and intuitive measure called the **total variation distance (TVD)**. This tells us, in a precise sense, how &quot;far apart&quot; two probability distributions are.<br>
<br>
### What is Total Variation Distance (TVD)?<br>
<br>
Let’s break this down. Suppose we have:<br>
- A **true** probability density function $f_{\theta_0}(y)$, which describes the real data-generating process.<br>
- An **estimated** density $f_\theta(y)$, based on our estimator using sample data.<br>
<br>
The total variation distance between these two densities is defined as:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\text{TVD}(\theta) = \int |f_\theta(y) - f_{\theta_0}(y)| \, dy<br>
$$<br>
<br>
🧠 **What does this mean intuitively?**  <br>
Imagine plotting both density curves on the same graph. At every point $y$, you look at the vertical gap (absolute difference) between the two curves. The TVD adds up all those gaps across the entire range of possible values. It&#x27;s like measuring the total &quot;area between the curves.&quot; <br>
<br>
✅ The smaller this area, the more similar the two distributions are.  <br>
✅ If the estimated density perfectly matches the truth, TVD = 0.  <br>
✅ The maximum possible value of TVD is 2, but since densities integrate to 1, the actual maximum difference is bounded by 1 in meaningful comparisons.<br>
<br>
So, **TVD quantifies the overall discrepancy between what we estimated and what is actually true**, making it an excellent global measure of estimation quality.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
### Measuring Estimator Performance: Mean Total Variation Distance (MTVD)<br>
<br>
Now, any estimator $\hat{\theta}$ (like maximum likelihood or a robust alternative) will give slightly different results each time due to random sampling. So we don’t just evaluate one estimate—we want to know how well the estimator performs *on average*.<br>
<br>
We define the **Mean Total Variation Distance (MTVD)** of an estimator $\hat{\theta}$ as:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\text{MTVD}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}\left[\text{TVD}(\hat{\theta})\right]<br>
$$<br>
<br>
📌 Here, the expectation $\mathbb{E}$ means: &quot;average over many repeated samples from the same true model.&quot;<br>
<br>
In other words, we simulate many datasets from the true model, apply our estimator to each, compute the TVD for each resulting estimate, and then take the average. This gives us a stable, reliable measure of how good the estimator is *in the long run*.<br>
<br>
Since we can’t compute this expectation exactly in practice, we approximate it using simulation. With $N$ simulated replications of the estimator $\hat{\theta}_k$ (for $k = 1, 2, \dots, N$), we calculate the **Average Total Variation Distance (ATVD)**:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\text{ATVD}(\hat{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N \text{TVD}(\hat{\theta}_k)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
This ATVD is our empirical estimate of MTVD. The larger $N$, the more accurate this approximation becomes.<br>
<br>
💡 **Why use TVD instead of something simpler like mean squared error?**  <br>
Because TVD evaluates the *entire shape* of the estimated density, not just specific moments. Two densities can have the same mean and variance but very different shapes (e.g., one bimodal, one unimodal). TVD catches such differences—making it a more comprehensive diagnostic tool.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
## 5.1.1 Simulation under the Nominal Model<br>
<br>
We now study how well the estimators perform when the data come exactly from the assumed model—the so-called **nominal model**. This is the &quot;best-case scenario&quot; where all assumptions hold.<br>
<br>
We considered sample sizes $n = 50, 100, 400,$ and $1000$, and true shape parameters $\lambda_0 = 1$ and $\lambda_0 = 2$. Without loss of generality (since location and scale can be standardized), we set the true location and scale parameters to $\mu_0 = 0$ and $\sigma_0 = 1$.<br>
<br>
We also examined two levels of **censoring**: 15% and 25%. Censoring occurs when some observations are only partially observed (e.g., in survival analysis, we know a patient survived up to a certain time, but not beyond). This adds complexity to estimation.<br>
<br>
For each configuration, we ran $N = 1200$ simulation replications to ensure stable estimates of ATVD.<br>
<br>
📊 **Figure 1 (top)** shows a particularly insightful comparison: it plots the **ATVD of robust estimators divided by the ATVD of the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimator**, as a function of sample size, for $\lambda = 1$ and 15% censoring.<br>
<br>
🔁 This ratio is called the **TVD efficiency**—a form of relative efficiency:<br>
- A value **less than 1** means the robust estimator performs *better* than ML (in terms of TVD).<br>
- A value **close to 1** means the robust estimator performs almost as well as ML.<br>
- A value **greater than 1** means it’s worse.<br>
<br>
🎯 **What do we expect?**  <br>
Under the nominal model (i.e., no outliers, perfect model fit), the ML estimator is theoretically optimal—it should achieve the lowest possible variance among consistent estimators. So we expect ML to have the smallest ATVD, meaning other estimators should have TVD efficiency ≥ 1.<br>
<br>
🔍 **What do we observe?**<br>
- The robust estimators—labeled 1TML, 2TML, and 1SWML—have TVD efficiencies **markedly greater than 1** for small samples (e.g., $n=50$), meaning they’re less efficient than ML when the model is correct.<br>
- But as the sample size increases, their TVD efficiency **approaches 1**. That is, they become nearly as good as ML in clean data.<br>
<br>
✅ This is excellent news for robust methods!  <br>
Even though they’re designed to handle contamination, they don’t pay too high a price in clean data—they are **asymptotically efficient**. As $n$ grows, they &quot;catch up&quot; to ML.<br>
<br>
🔄 Similar patterns were observed across other settings ($\lambda_0 = 2$, 25% censoring), confirming that this behavior is consistent.<br>
<br>
📌 **Takeaway**: These robust estimators strike a smart balance—they may sacrifice a bit of efficiency in ideal conditions, but they gain resilience when things go wrong (as we’ll see next).<br>
<br>

## 5.1.2 Simulation under Point Mass Contamination<br>
<br>
Now let’s stress-test the estimators. What happens when the data aren’t perfect? In real-world applications, datasets often contain **outliers**—erroneous or extreme observations that don’t follow the assumed model.<br>
<br>
To simulate this, we conducted a second Monte Carlo experiment with **point mass contamination**. This is a classic and challenging way to introduce outliers.<br>
<br>
Here’s how it works:<br>
- 90% of the data (“good” observations) are generated from the true Generalized Log-Gamma (GLG) model.<br>
- 10% of the data (“outliers”) are placed at a fixed point $y_0$, creating a spike (or “point mass”) in the distribution.<br>
<br>
We varied $y_0$ from $-10$ to $10$ in steps of $0.5$, effectively sliding the outlier cluster across the real line to test how sensitive each estimator is to the location of contamination.<br>
<br>
🎯 **Why use point mass contamination?**  <br>
It’s considered one of the **most adverse forms of contamination** because it creates a sharp, concentrated deviation from the model. If an estimator can withstand this, it’s likely to perform well under more diffuse or realistic outlier scenarios.<br>
<br>
We again used $n = 50, 100, 400, 1000$, $\lambda_0 = 1$ and $2$, and censoring fractions of 15% and 25%. For each value of $y_0$, we performed 1200 replications.<br>
<br>
📊 **Figure 1 (bottom)** displays the **ATVD of the estimated densities** as a function of $y_0$. This shows how the overall estimation error changes depending on where the outliers are located.<br>
<br>
🔍 **Key Findings**:<br>
- The estimators **TQ$\tau$ (with 10% trimming), 1TML, and 2TML** show remarkably **stable ATVD values** across all values of $y_0$. Their performance barely wavers whether outliers are on the far left ($y_0 = -10$), center, or far right ($y_0 = 10$).<br>
- In contrast, the **1SWL estimator** exhibits a **very high ATVD for positive values of $y_0$**—indicating it breaks down badly when outliers appear in the upper tail.<br>
- The **ML estimator** is so sensitive to outliers that its ATVD becomes extremely large—so large, in fact, that it’s **not shown** in the plot. Its behavior is **unbounded**: a single outlier can completely distort the ML estimate.<br>
<br>
🧠 **Why does ML fail so badly?**  <br>
Maximum likelihood assumes every data point comes from the model. When even 10% of the data are wildly off, ML tries to &quot;explain&quot; them by drastically shifting the estimated parameters. This leads to massive bias.<br>
<br>
🛠️ **How do the robust estimators resist this?**<br>
- **TQ$\tau$** uses trimming: it automatically down-weights or removes the most extreme 10% of residuals, effectively ignoring the outliers.<br>
- **1TML and 2TML** are two-step robust procedures: they first identify and reduce the influence of outlying observations before re-estimating the model.<br>
- **1SWL**, unfortunately, lacks sufficient protection against asymmetric contamination, especially in the upper tail, which explains its poor performance.<br>
<br>
📌 **Takeaway**: Under contamination, robustness matters. While ML fails catastrophically, properly designed robust estimators maintain stable, reliable performance—exactly what we need in practical data analysis.<br>

## 5.2 Regression Case<br>
<br>
We now extend our evaluation to a more complex and realistic setting: **regression with censored responses**.<br>
<br>
A second set of experiments was run to investigate the behavior of:<br>
- The **MM-TQ$\tau$** estimator (defined in Section 4.1),<br>
- The **1TML** and **2TML** estimators (defined in Section 4.2),<br>
- And the **1SWL** estimator (defined in Section A.3.2 of the Appendix).<br>
<br>
In each simulation, we generated $n$ pairs of observations $(x_i, y^*_i)$ according to the following regression model:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
y_i = \mu_0 + \beta_0^\top x_i + \sigma_0 u_i, \tag{34}<br>
$$<br>
$$<br>
y^*_i = \min(y_i, c_i), \tag{35}<br>
$$<br>
$$<br>
c_i = \mu_c + e_i, \tag{36}<br>
$$<br>
$$<br>
\delta_i = <br>
\begin{cases} <br>
1 &amp; \text{if } y^*_i = y_i \\<br>
0 &amp; \text{otherwise}<br>
\end{cases} \tag{37}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Let’s unpack this step by step.<br>
<br>
📌 **Equation (34)**: This is the **true regression model**.<br>
- $y_i$: the latent (unobserved) response variable.<br>
- $x_i = (x_{i1}, x_{i2})$: a vector of two predictors.<br>
- $\beta_0^\top = (2, 3)$: the true regression coefficients.<br>
- $u_i$: random error drawn from a GLG distribution with parameters $(\mu=0, \sigma=1, \lambda=\lambda_0)$, where $\lambda_0 = 1$ or $2$.<br>
- $\mu_0 = 0$, $\sigma_0 = 1$: location and scale of the error term.<br>
<br>
📌 **Equation (35)**: This introduces **right-censoring**.<br>
- $y^*_i$: the observed response.<br>
- $c_i$: the censoring threshold.<br>
- If the true $y_i$ exceeds $c_i$, we only observe $c_i$, not the full value. This mimics scenarios like survival times where a patient is still alive at the end of the study.<br>
<br>
📌 **Equation (36)**: The censoring threshold $c_i$ itself varies across individuals:<br>
- $\mu_c$: a fixed baseline.<br>
- $e_i \sim \text{GLG}(\mu_c, 1, 1)$: random variation in censoring times.<br>
<br>
📌 **Equation (37)**: The indicator $\delta_i$ tells us whether observation $i$ is censored ($\delta_i = 0$) or fully observed ($\delta_i = 1$).<br>
<br>
🎯 **Goal**: Estimate the regression parameters $(\mu_0, \beta_0, \sigma_0, \lambda_0)$ accurately despite censoring and potential outliers.<br>
<br>

- $x_{i1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$: a continuous covariate (standard normal),<br>
- $x_{i2} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.4)$: a binary covariate (e.g., treatment vs. control),<br>
- Independent of each other.<br>
