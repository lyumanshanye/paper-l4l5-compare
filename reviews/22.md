**Introduction**<br>
<br>
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition in which the body’s response to infection damages its own tissues and organs. In Brazil, the mortality rate for sepsis is known to be higher in public hospitals than in private ones, even though patients in both settings appear to have similar risk factors. This observation raises a critical question: *Why* does this difference exist? The goal of this study is to identify specific risk factors—particularly those related to the timing and quality of care—that might explain the mortality gap between public and private healthcare institutions.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
**Methods**<br>
<br>
To investigate this, the researchers conducted a prospective study (meaning they followed patients forward in time, collecting data as events unfolded). They included all adult patients (over 18 years old) with severe sepsis or septic shock who were admitted to one of three intensive care units (ICUs): two private hospitals (Group 1) and one public hospital (Group 2).<br>
<br>
For each patient, they collected a wide range of data: demographic information (age, gender), clinical details (severity of illness, number of organ dysfunctions), and outcome data (whether the patient survived). They also tracked **compliance with the 6-hour Surviving Sepsis Campaign bundle (SSC-6h)** — a set of evidence-based interventions that should be completed within six hours of recognizing sepsis, including measuring blood lactate, taking blood cultures, giving antibiotics, and maintaining blood pressure.<br>
<br>
One particularly important variable was the **duration of organ dysfunction**. This was defined as the time elapsed between the *onset* of an organ dysfunction and the moment it was *diagnosed* by a healthcare provider. This measure is critical because it captures not just *how sick* a patient is, but *how quickly* the system recognizes and responds to that sickness.<br>
<br>
Results were expressed as percentages (for categorical data) or as medians with interquartile ranges (for continuous data, since these are often not normally distributed). A *P* value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, meaning the result is unlikely to be due to chance.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
**Results**<br>
<br>
A total of 386 patients were enrolled: 210 in Group 1 (private) and 176 in Group 2 (public).<br>
<br>
At first glance, the private hospital patients (Group 1) appeared *sicker*: they were older (median 69.5 years vs. 59.0 years, *P* &lt; 0.000), had higher APACHE II scores (a measure of acute illness severity, 19.0 vs. 17.0, *P* = 0.01), and had more organ dysfunctions (3.0 vs. 2.0, *P* = 0.08). They also had a higher proportion of septic shock episodes (74.3% vs. 60.8%, *P* = 0.006). There were no significant differences in gender, site of infection, or the change in SOFA score (a measure of organ dysfunction) from day 1 to day 3.<br>
<br>
Despite this, the **mortality rate was dramatically higher in the public hospital (Group 2)**: 60.8% vs. 38.3% in the private hospitals. This is a striking difference — nearly 23 percentage points — and it is *not* explained by the patients being sicker at admission.<br>
<br>
In fact, the public hospital actually had **better compliance** with the SSC-6h bundle for several key measures: lactate measurement (70.5% vs. 49.8%), blood culture collection (61.4% vs. 52.4%), achieving a mean arterial pressure (MAP) &gt;65 mmHg (98.8% vs. 90.5%), and completing the entire bundle (15.9% vs. 4.8%). Only antibiotic administration (63.3% vs. 54.5%, *P* = 0.10) and central venous pressure (CVP) measurement (49.4% vs. 33.3%, *P* = 0.01) were better in the private group.<br>
<br>
So, if the public hospital is doing *more* of the right things, why are its patients dying more often?<br>
<br>
The answer may lie in **timing**. The SOFA score at admission was slightly higher in Group 2 (8.0 vs. 7.0, *P* = 0.004), indicating a marginally worse initial organ function. But the most telling finding was the **duration of organ dysfunction**: it was clearly longer in the public hospital — 360.0 minutes (median) vs. 150.0 minutes in the private hospitals (*P* = 0.02). In other words, organ dysfunction was present for more than twice as long before being recognized in the public setting.<br>
<br>
When the researchers looked at all patients together, they identified several variables associated with mortality: being treated in a public hospital (odds ratio 2.46, 95% CI 1.63–3.72, *P* = 0.00002), the median duration of organ dysfunction, SOFA at admission, age, APACHE II score, and the number of organ dysfunctions.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
**Conclusions**<br>
<br>
The mortality rate was higher in the public hospital despite better adherence to the SSC-6h bundle. This suggests that the *quality* of care is not the only issue — the *speed* of recognition and intervention matters enormously. A delay in initiating those interventions may negatively impact the outcome in patients treated in public hospitals.</div></td>

## Introduction<br>
<br>
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition that occurs when the body’s response to an infection spirals out of control, damaging its own tissues and organs. If not treated quickly, sepsis can progress to septic shock, organ failure, and death. In Brazil, studies have shown that patients with sepsis in public hospitals face higher mortality rates compared to those in private hospitals—even when their initial risk factors appear similar. This raises a critical question: **Why do more patients die from sepsis in public hospitals if the patients don’t seem inherently sicker at first glance?**<br>
<br>
The goal of this study was to dig deeper. The researchers wanted to find out whether differences in hospital systems—like how quickly care is delivered or how strictly treatment guidelines are followed—could explain this gap in survival. Specifically, they focused on the **Surviving Sepsis Campaign 6-hour bundle (SSC-6h)**, a set of time-sensitive medical actions proven to improve outcomes when completed within six hours of recognizing sepsis.<br>
<br>
But here’s the key insight: **It’s not just about *whether* these actions are done—it’s about *how fast* they’re started.** Even if two hospitals follow the same checklist, a delay in beginning treatment could mean the difference between life and death. So, the team set out to compare patients in public and private ICUs, not only looking at survival rates and guideline adherence but also measuring how long it took for organ dysfunction to be recognized and acted upon.<br>
<br>
## Methods<br>
<br>
The study included all adult patients (over 18 years old) diagnosed with severe sepsis or septic shock who were admitted to the intensive care units (ICUs) of three hospitals in São Paulo, Brazil. Two of these hospitals were private (Group 1), and one was public (Group 2). Every patient was enrolled prospectively, meaning data were collected in real time as patients came in, reducing the risk of recall bias or missing information.<br>
<br>
For each patient, the researchers gathered detailed information, including:<br>
- Basic demographics (age, gender)<br>
- Clinical features (source of infection, severity of illness)<br>
- Outcomes (survival, length of stay, organ dysfunction)<br>
- Adherence to the **SSC-6h bundle**, which includes six critical steps:<br>
  1. Measuring blood lactate levels<br>
  2. Obtaining blood cultures before antibiotics<br>
  3. Administering broad-spectrum antibiotics<br>
  4. Giving IV fluids for low blood pressure<br>
  5. Achieving a mean arterial pressure (MAP) &gt;65 mmHg, using fluids or medications<br>
  6. Achieving central venous pressure (CVP) of 8–12 mmHg (or 12–15 if on mechanical ventilation)<br>
<br>
All this data was recorded in a secure electronic case report form (e-CRF), ensuring consistency and accuracy.<br>
<br>
One particularly important measure was the **duration of organ dysfunction**—defined as the time between when organ failure *actually began* and when it was *recognized and documented* by the healthcare team. Why does this matter? Because the sooner doctors realize an organ is failing (like the lungs, kidneys, or liver), the faster they can intervene. A long delay here could mean the body is under stress for hours without treatment, worsening the patient’s condition even if the formal checklist is eventually completed.<br>
<br>
Results were analyzed using standard statistical methods. Categorical data (like percentages of patients receiving antibiotics) were compared using proportions. Continuous data (like age or APACHE II scores) were reported as **median and interquartile range (IQR)**—this is more reliable than averages when data are skewed (for example, a few very old patients could distort the average age). A *p-value* less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, meaning the observed difference is unlikely due to random chance.<br>
<br>
## Results<br>
<br>
A total of **386 patients** were included in the study: 210 from private hospitals (Group 1) and 176 from the public hospital (Group 2).<br>
<br>
At first glance, you might expect patients in private hospitals to be healthier or less severely ill. But surprisingly, the opposite was true. Patients in Group 1 (private) were actually **older**—median age 69.5 years (IQR: 53.0–81.0)—compared to 59.0 years (IQR: 43.5–73.5) in the public hospital (Group 2), and this difference was highly significant (*P &lt; 0.000*). They also had higher illness severity scores:<br>
<br>
- **APACHE II score**: 19.0 (15.0–23.0) vs. 17.0 (13.0–23.0), *P = 0.01*  <br>
  *(APACHE II is a scoring system that predicts the risk of death based on vital signs, lab values, and medical history—the higher the score, the sicker the patient.)*<br>
- More **organ dysfunctions** (3.0 vs. 2.0, *P = 0.08*)—though this didn’t quite reach statistical significance.<br>
- More cases of **septic shock** (74.3% vs. 60.8%, *P = 0.006*)<br>
<br>
In other words, the private hospital patients started out in worse shape—older, sicker, more likely to be in shock—yet they had a **much lower mortality rate**: **38.3%** in private hospitals versus **60.8%** in the public hospital. That’s a dramatic difference.<br>
<br>
Now, you might wonder: Could this survival advantage be explained by better adherence to the SSC-6h bundle in private hospitals? After all, following best practices should improve outcomes.<br>
<br>
But here’s the twist: **The public hospital actually did better on most parts of the bundle.**<br>
<br>
Let’s break it down:<br>
- **Lactate measurement**: 70.5% (public) vs. 49.8% (private) ✅<br>
- **Blood cultures before antibiotics**: 61.4% vs. 52.4% ✅<br>
- **Achieving MAP &gt;65 mmHg**: 98.8% vs. 90.5% ✅<br>
- **Overall bundle completion**: 15.9% vs. 4.8% ✅<br>
<br>
All of these were higher in the public hospital. So, despite doing *more* of the recommended actions, they had *worse* survival.<br>
<br>
Only two elements favored the private hospitals:<br>
- **Antibiotics administered within 6 hours**: 63.3% vs. 54.5% (*P = 0.10*)—this difference wasn’t statistically significant.<br>
- **CVP monitoring**: 49.4% vs. 33.3% (*P = 0.01*)—this one was significantly better in private hospitals.<br>
<br>
So, bundle compliance alone doesn’t explain the mortality gap. In fact, the public hospital outperformed the private ones on most metrics.<br>
<br>
But then what does explain it?<br>
<br>
Two other findings stand out:<br>
<br>
1. **SOFA score at admission** was slightly higher in the public hospital: 8.0 (5.5–10.0) vs. 7.0 (4.0–9.0), *P = 0.004*.  <br>
   *(The SOFA score—Sequential Organ Failure Assessment—measures how many organs are failing and how badly. A higher score means more severe organ dysfunction at the time of ICU admission.)*  <br>
   This suggests that by the time patients arrived in the ICU, those in the public hospital were already a bit more unstable.<br>
<br>
2. More importantly: **Duration of organ dysfunction** was significantly longer in the public hospital:  <br>
   360.0 minutes (0.0 to 1008.0) vs. 150.0 minutes (0.0 to 650.0), *P = 0.02*.  <br>
   That’s a median delay of **6 hours** in the public system versus **2.5 hours** in the private system.<br>
<br>
Think about that: **Patients in the public hospital had failing organs for, on average, 3.5 hours longer before the dysfunction was recognized and documented.** This delay likely meant that life-saving treatments—like fluids, antibiotics, or vasopressors—were started later, even if they were eventually given.<br>
<br>
Finally, the researchers ran a statistical model to identify which factors were independently associated with death. They found that:<br>
- Being treated in a **public hospital** carried a 2.46 times higher odds of death (OR 2.46, 95% CI: 1.63–3.72, *P = 0.00002*)<br>
- Other strong predictors included:<br>
  - Longer **duration of organ dysfunction**<br>
  - Higher **SOFA score at admission**<br>
  - Older **age**<br>
  - Higher **APACHE II score**<br>
  - Greater **number of organ dysfunctions**<br>
<br>
This confirms that hospital type—public vs. private—is a powerful predictor of survival, even after accounting for how sick the patient is.<br>
<br>
## Conclusions<br>
<br>
The mortality rate was higher in the public hospital despite better adherence to the SSC-6h bundle. This counterintuitive finding reveals a crucial insight: **It’s not just about completing the checklist—it’s about *when* you start.**</div></td>
