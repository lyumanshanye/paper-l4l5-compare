This table dives deeper using **gastric emptying scintigraphy (GES)**—a nuclear medicine test that objectively measures how fast the stomach empties.<br>
<br>
Here’s how it works: the patient eats a meal containing a small amount of radioactive tracer. A camera then tracks how much radioactivity remains in the stomach over time. It’s like putting a GPS tracker on food.<br>
<br>
Key metrics:<br>
- **Lag time**: How long the stomach waits before starting to empty. Think of it as the “warm-up” phase.<br>
- **t₁/₂ (half-emptying time)**: The time it takes for half the stomach contents to empty. A longer t₁/₂ means slower emptying.<br>
- **%RA120**: The percentage of radioactivity (i.e., food) still in the stomach after 120 minutes (2 hours). Higher values mean more food is stuck.<br>
<br>
Let’s look at **preoperative** scans first:<br>
- Both groups had similar baseline function. Lag time, t₁/₂, and %RA120 were nearly identical.<br>
- This confirms that before surgery, stomach motility was comparable—so any post-op differences are likely due to the surgery itself.<br>
<br>
Now, **postoperative** results:<br>
- **Lag time**: Increased dramatically in both groups (as expected after major surgery), but the retrocolic group had a much longer median lag time (66 min vs. 22 min). That’s a big difference—over 3 times longer. However, the P-value is 0.141, not statistically significant. Why? Because there’s high variability (some patients emptied quickly, others very slowly), which makes it harder to detect a true effect.<br>
  <br>
- **t₁/₂**: Again, much longer in retrocolic (145 min vs. 64 min). That means it took more than twice as long to empty half the stomach. But P = 0.189—not significant.<br>
<br>
- **%RA120**: After 2 hours, the retrocolic group retained 64% of the meal on average, compared to 28% in antecolic. That’s a striking difference. More than half still in the stomach vs. less than a third. And 12 patients in retrocolic had &gt;60% retention (a common threshold for abnormal emptying), versus only 7 in antecolic.<br>
<br>
Even though none of these differences reach statistical significance (likely due to small sample size and high variation), the **trend is clear and consistent**: the **retrocolic group shows markedly slower gastric emptying** after surgery.<br>
<br>
This is a powerful example of how **objective testing can reveal hidden dysfunction** that clinical observation might miss. Patients may have been discharged and eating adequately, yet their stomachs were still struggling to process food efficiently.<br>
<br>
So while both techniques look similar in terms of overt complications (Table 2), the physiological impact—measured precisely—suggests **antecolic reconstruction may preserve better stomach motility**.<br>
