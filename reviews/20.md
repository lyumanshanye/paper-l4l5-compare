<td valign="top"><div>When analyzing the results of ultrasonographic measurements (Table 3), lambs of the experimental group showed better muscle tissue development compared to those in the control group. Specifically, the cross-sectional area of the *m. longissimus dorsi* (often abbreviated as m.l.d.)—a key muscle located along the back, commonly used as an indicator of meat yield in livestock—was 17.74% larger in the experimental group on day 28 of life and still 10.46% larger on day 70. This suggests that the lambs receiving the supplement were building more muscle earlier and maintaining that advantage over time.<br>
<br>
Now, let’s pause and think: why would we care about the m.l.d. area? In meat-producing animals like sheep, the size of this muscle is directly related to carcass value. A larger m.l.d. area generally means more high-quality meat, especially in premium cuts like the loin. So, even though the difference wasn’t statistically significant, the consistent trend toward greater muscle development in the experimental group is promising—it hints that the intervention might be supporting better growth at the tissue level.<br>
<br>
However, when it came to fat thickness above the “loin eye”—another common ultrasonographic measurement used to assess meat quality and marbling—no noticeable differences were found between the two groups. This tells us that while muscle development appeared to benefit, fat deposition did not change significantly. That’s actually useful information: it means the treatment may be selectively influencing muscle growth without altering fat accumulation, which could be desirable depending on breeding or market goals.<br>
<br>
Moving on to immune function, the data in Table 4 reveal a clearer and more statistically robust story. In ewes that were given β-1,3/1,6-D-glucan, several markers of immunity were elevated compared to the control group. Let’s unpack what this means.<br>
<br>
First, the study looked at **non-specific humoral immunity**, which refers to the body’s general, first-line immune defenses found in bodily fluids (the “humors,” like blood and mucus). Two key markers here were:<br>
<br>
- **Gammaglobulin concentration**: These are proteins in the blood that include antibodies—molecules that help neutralize pathogens like bacteria and viruses. Higher levels suggest a stronger readiness to fight infection.<br>
- **Lysozyme activity**: Lysozyme is an enzyme that can directly break down the cell walls of certain bacteria, acting like a built-in antibacterial agent. Increased activity means the ewes’ bodies were more capable of destroying microbes on contact.<br>
<br>
In addition to these fluid-based defenses, the study also measured **markers of cellular immunity**—which involve specialized white blood cells (like T-cells and macrophages) that identify and destroy infected or abnormal cells. While the specific cellular markers aren’t named here, the fact that they were collectively higher in the experimental group indicates a more active and responsive immune system at the cellular level.<br>
<br>
The most important takeaway? The differences in these immune markers weren’t just slight—they were **statistically significant at the P &lt; 0.01 level**. That means there’s less than a 1% probability that these results happened by chance. In scientific terms, this is a strong signal that β-1,3/1,6-D-glucan genuinely boosted the ewes’ immune function.<br>
<br>
So, what’s the bigger picture here? This immune enhancement likely contributed to the improved mammary health observed in the experimental group, as reflected in the lower somatic cell count (SCC) in their milk—a point we’ll return to in the discussion. But for now, it’s worth noting that β-1,3/1,6-D-glucan appears to act as an **immunomodulator**: a substance that doesn’t directly kill pathogens but instead &quot;primes&quot; the immune system to respond more effectively when threats arise.<br>
<br>
This principle of immune priming is actually a cornerstone of modern animal health strategies, especially in reducing reliance on antibiotics. By boosting natural defenses, such supplements can help prevent infections before they start—particularly important in high-stress periods like lactation.<br>
<br>
Now, let’s look at the earlier tables to see how these immune and developmental benefits connect with actual performance.<br>
<br>
**Table 1** shows the effect of β-1,3/1,6-D-glucan on milk production and composition. Right away, we see that milk yield was significantly higher in the experimental group: 1812 mL vs. 1596 mL on day 28 (P &lt; 0.05), and 1139 mL vs. 998 mL on day 70. That’s a meaningful increase—especially on day 28, when lambs are growing rapidly and depend heavily on milk.<br>
<br>
But it’s not just about quantity. The **quality** of the milk also improved. Fat content was significantly higher—6.56% vs. 5.04% on day 28 and 8.13% vs. 7.05% on day 70 (P &lt; 0.01). Since fat is a major source of energy for growing lambs, this means each sip of milk provided more fuel for growth. Similarly, protein content increased significantly from day 28 to 70 (6.16% vs. 5.55%, P &lt; 0.05), delivering more building blocks for muscle and tissue development.<br>
<br>
Even the **dry matter** content—the total solids in milk, including fat, protein, lactose, and minerals—was significantly higher in the experimental group at both time points. This further confirms that the milk was more nutrient-dense.<br>
<br>
One of the most striking findings, though, is in the **somatic cell count (SCC)**. On day 28, the control group had an average of 316,770 cells per mL, while the experimental group had only 96,230—a dramatic difference (P &lt; 0.01). High SCC is a classic indicator of inflammation or infection in the udder, often due to mastitis. So, this sharp reduction strongly suggests that the supplemented ewes had healthier mammary glands.<br>
<br>
Why does this matter? Because mastitis doesn’t just make animals sick—it directly harms milk quality. It can alter pH, reduce shelf life, and interfere with cheese-making and other processing. So, lowering SCC isn’t just a health win; it’s an economic and quality win too.<br>
<br>
Now, looking at **Table 2**, we see how these improvements in milk quantity and quality translated to the lambs. Body weight at birth was nearly identical between groups—about 4.91 kg—so any later differences can’t be blamed on initial size. But by day 28, lambs in the experimental group averaged 12.16 kg vs. 11.84 kg in the control group, and by day 70, they reached 20.15 kg vs. 19.75 kg. That’s a consistent, though modest, advantage.<br>
<br>
Daily weight gains followed the same pattern: slightly higher in the experimental group across all periods (2–28, 28–70, and 2–70 days), though none of the differences reached statistical significance. The same goes for growth rates, which were very similar between groups.<br>
<br>
So, while the lambs did grow a bit faster and end up a bit heavier—likely thanks to better milk nutrition—the real story isn’t in dramatic weight gains. Instead, it’s in the **quality of growth**: more muscle, better immune priming from the mother’s milk, and a healthier start in life.<br>
<br>
This leads us into the **Discussion**, where the authors begin to connect these dots.<br>
