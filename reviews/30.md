## Wavelet Transforms in Euclidean Spaces<br>
<br>
Let&#x27;s begin by setting the stage. We are in the middle of a proof—specifically, the proof of Theorem 1—and we have reached what the author calls &quot;the heart of the matter.&quot; Up to this point, we have established some preliminary properties of the wavelet transform. Now, we need to prove a crucial technical fact that will unlock the theorem.<br>
<br>
**Setting up the final piece.** We define \( U_2(0) = \{\phi(x) \equiv 1\} \). In plain terms, this means we are considering the constant function that is equal to 1 everywhere. Why do we care about this? Because we want to show that a certain wavelet transform behaves nicely—specifically, that it belongs to a *Sobolev space*, which is a space of functions that have a certain amount of smoothness and decay. This is a very strong and useful property.<br>
<br>
Now, by applying Proposition 2 (which we have already established) and by exchanging the order of differentiation and integration—a step that is justified here because our functions are well-behaved enough—we can easily verify that the transform has the desired property. The exchange of differentiation and integration is a standard trick: when we want to differentiate an integral, we can often move the derivative inside the integral sign, provided the integrand is sufficiently smooth and decays nicely. This is exactly what we do here.<br>
<br>
So, we have shown that the transform is smooth in a certain sense. But we want more. We want to show that the transform belongs *globally* to the Sobolev space \(H^s(\mathbb{R}^n)\). This means that not only is the function smooth, but its derivatives up to order \(s\) are also square-integrable over the entire space. This is a strong regularity result.<br>
<br>
To prove this, we turn to the **Fourier transform**, which is a powerful tool for studying smoothness. The Fourier transform of our wavelet transform is given by an explicit formula. Let&#x27;s write it down and then unpack it.<br>
<br>
\[<br>
\widehat{f_{\phi&#x27;,\phi}}(\xi) = \int g(\xi, \eta) \overline{\hat{\phi}(\eta)} \, d\eta<br>
\]<br>
<br>
Here, the notation \(\widehat{\cdot}\) denotes the Fourier transform. The key idea is that the Fourier transform converts differentiation into multiplication, so studying the decay of the Fourier transform tells us about smoothness.<br>
<br>
Now, we make a clever substitution. We define a new variable, say \( \eta = r \theta \), where \( r &gt; 0 \) and \( \theta \in S^{n-1} \) (the unit sphere in \( \mathbb{R}^n \)). This is a standard trick: we decompose the frequency space into radial and angular parts. The hypothesis of our theorem, combined with the fact that the support of \( \phi(x) \) is contained in the unit ball \( U(0) \), gives us a crucial bound. Let&#x27;s unpack that.<br>
<br>
The **support** of a function is the set of points where it is nonzero. Since \( \phi \) is supported inside the unit ball, its Fourier transform \( \hat{\phi} \) has certain decay properties. This is a fundamental fact: the more localized a function is in space, the more spread out its Fourier transform is, and vice versa. Here, the compact support of \( \phi \) means that \( \hat{\phi} \) is an entire function of exponential type, which gives us strong decay estimates.<br>
<br>
So, we have the inequality:<br>
<br>
\[<br>
| \widehat{f}(\xi) | \le C \int |g(\xi, \eta)| \, |\hat{\phi}(\eta)| \, d\eta<br>
\]<br>
<br>
where \( C \) is a constant. Now, we denote the Fourier transform of \( g(x, \eta) \) with respect to \( x \) by \( \hat{g}(\xi, \eta) \). This is a standard notation: the hat over \( g \) indicates that we have taken the Fourier transform in the first variable only, leaving the second variable untouched.<br>
<br>
We then define a kernel \( K(\xi, \eta) \) by:<br>
<br>
\[<br>
K(\xi, \eta) = \hat{g}(\xi, \eta) \overline{\hat{\phi}(\eta)}<br>
\]<br>
<br>
This kernel is the product of the Fourier transform of \( g \) and the conjugate of the Fourier transform of \( \phi \). It&#x27;s a measure of how much the wavelet transform &quot;picks up&quot; at frequency \( \xi \) from the wavelet at scale \( \eta \).<br>
<br>
Now, here&#x27;s the key observation. Because the support of \( \phi \) is compact, there exists a constant \( C \) such that:<br>
<br>
\[<br>
|K(\xi, \eta)| \le C<br>
\]<br>
<br>
This is a uniform bound—it doesn&#x27;t depend on \( \xi \) or \( \eta \). This is a consequence of the fact that \( \hat{\phi} \) is bounded (since \( \phi \) is integrable) and that \( \hat{g} \) is also bounded (by the hypothesis of the theorem).<br>
<br>
Now, since \( 0 \notin \text{supp} \, \hat{\phi} \)—that is, the Fourier transform of \( \phi \) does not vanish at the origin—we can use the same argument as in the proof of Proposition 1. The integral over \( \eta \) is bounded by a constant times the \( L^2 \) norm of \( g \). Specifically, we get:<br>
<br>
\[<br>
\int |K(\xi, \eta)| \, d\eta \le (2\pi)^{-n} C \| \hat{\phi} \|_2^2<br>
\]<br>
<br>
This is a crucial estimate. It tells us that the kernel is integrable in \( \eta \), uniformly in \( \xi \). This allows us to bound the Fourier transform of our wavelet transform.<br>
<br>
Consequently, we obtain the following inequality:<br>
<br>
\[<br>
\| \hat{f} \|_{L^2} \le C \| g \|_{L^2}<br>
\]<br>
<br>
which, by the Plancherel theorem (which says that the Fourier transform is an isometry on \( L^2 \)), gives us the desired Sobolev space membership. This completes the proof of the theorem.<br>
<br>
**q.e.d.**<br>

<br>
For simplicity, we introduce some notation. Let:<br>
<br>
\[<br>

where \( d\theta \) is the Haar measure on the unit sphere \( S^{n-1} \). The Haar measure is the natural notion of &quot;uniform&quot; measure on a group or a sphere—it&#x27;s the measure that is invariant under rotations.<br>
<br>
Now, because the support of \( \phi \) is compact and does not contain the origin, the function \( \phi_r(\theta) \) is compactly supported in the domain:<br>
<br>
\[<br>
C_1 r \le |\theta| \le C_2 r<br>
\]<br>
<br>
This is a spherical shell—a region between two spheres of radii \( C_1 r \) and \( C_2 r \). This is a direct consequence of the fact that \( \phi \) is supported away from zero, so when we scale it by \( r \), the support moves outward.<br>
<br>

<br>
\[<br>
\| f \|_{B^s_{p,q}} \le C \| f \|_{B^s_{p,q}(\psi)}<br>

<br>
The first inequality is due to the **continuous version of the Minkowski inequality**, which is a generalization of the triangle inequality to integrals. It allows us to bound the norm of a sum by the sum of the norms. The second inequality is due to the **Hölder inequality**, which is a fundamental tool for bounding products of functions.<br>
<br>

This is a matter of unpacking the definitions: the Besov norm is defined via a weighted integral over scales, and the wavelet transform provides a natural way to measure the size of the function at each scale.<br>

\]<br>

where \( f_j \) is the piece of \( f \) whose Fourier transform is supported in the \( j \)-th frequency band.<br>

Why does this matter? Because Sobolev spaces measure not just the size of a function, but also the size of its derivatives, giving us a precise way to quantify smoothness. This is crucial when we want to understand how well a function can be approximated or reconstructed from its wavelet coefficients.<br>

From Proposition 2, we know that under suitable conditions, we can exchange the order of differentiation and integration. This might sound technical, but here&#x27;s what it means intuitively: if you&#x27;re integrating a function that also depends on a parameter (like scale or position in wavelet analysis), and you want to take a derivative with respect to that parameter, sometimes you can safely &quot;pull&quot; the derivative inside the integral.
