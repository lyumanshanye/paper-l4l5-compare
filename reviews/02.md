<br>
Let’s pause and unpack that timeline, because it’s the backbone of the entire study design. The researchers have carefully budgeted every minute of the encounter. Why? Because they want to ensure that every participant—whether they are in the telehealth or in-person condition—has exactly the same experience. This is what scientists call *standardization*: if you want to compare two groups fairly, you must hold everything constant except the one thing you’re testing (in this case, the delivery mode). So, the total interaction is capped at one hour, and within that hour, the structure is fixed: a brief orientation to get the participant comfortable and informed, a 30-minute window for the actual clinical interaction (the heart of the exercise), an 8-minute feedback session where the standardized patient (SP) shares their observations, and a final 5–10 minute debrief with the facilitator. <br>
<br>
Upon arrival to the interaction, participants are introduced to the procedures and are given a brief written backstory about the SP scenario. Backstories provide context for the provider and include details about the patient’s name, age, the nature of the patient-provider relationship, some medical and social history and the reason for this visit (“trouble sleeping”). <br>
<br>
Here, the backstory is not just a nice-to-have; it’s a deliberate tool. Think of it as a script for the opening scene of a play. The participant (the provider) needs to know who they are walking in to see, what the patient’s history is, and why the patient is here. The reason for the visit is given as “trouble sleeping,” which is a classic presenting complaint that can mask a deeper issue—like suicidality. This is a clever design choice: it forces the participant to look beyond the surface and practice their clinical interviewing skills to uncover what’s really going on.<br>
<br>
Telehealth participants also receive instructions about remote access. <br>
<br>
This is a small but vital detail. If you’re doing the interaction over video, you need to know how to log in, how to adjust your camera, and how to troubleshoot technical issues. The researchers are making sure that the technology doesn’t become a confounding variable—that is, they don’t want a participant’s performance to be affected by a bad internet connection or confusion about the software. They want the only difference between groups to be the *mode* of interaction, not the *quality* of the experience.<br>
<br>
Participants are instructed to assess the patient’s risk for suicidality and provide appropriate interventions within a 30-minute timeframe (a timer signals the appointment end). <br>
<br>
This is the heart of the test. The participant is given a clear, measurable goal: assess the risk, and then act on that assessment. The 30-minute limit is a pressure test. In real clinical practice, you don’t have unlimited time with a patient, so this constraint mimics the real-world urgency. The timer is a silent but powerful presence—it forces the participant to prioritize, to be efficient, and to make sure they hit the key points of a suicide risk assessment (e.g., asking directly about suicidal thoughts, assessing intent and plan, and creating a safety plan if needed).<br>
<br>
After the interaction, participants exit the room for 10 min during which time the SP prepares immediate feedback (positive and constructive) for the patient. <br>
<br>
Notice the careful choreography here. The participant leaves, giving the SP a quiet, uninterrupted window to collect their thoughts. This is not a casual chat; it’s a structured feedback session. The SP is not just an actor; they are also an evaluator, trained to observe specific behaviors and to report back on them.<br>
<br>
SPs are highly trained to prepare and deliver tailored feedback using a 15-item “feedback matrix” template of criteria for identifying appropriate feedback items. <br>
<br>
This matrix is a checklist of competencies. It’s like a rubric for a performance review. The SP uses it to ensure that their feedback is not vague (“you did well”) but specific and tied to the skills that the training program is designed to teach. For example, the matrix might include items like “asked about suicidal ideation directly” or “provided a safety plan.” This ensures that the feedback is consistent across all participants, regardless of which SP they interacted with.<br>
<br>
Feedback includes specific competency items with explanations and rationale for the importance of the skill. <br>
<br>
This is a key pedagogical point: feedback is not just about *what* you did, but *why* it matters. The SP explains, for instance, “You asked if the patient had a plan, which is crucial because it helps you gauge the immediacy of the risk.” This helps the participant understand the underlying clinical reasoning, not just memorize a checklist.<br>
<br>
The participant joins the SP for 8 min to receive feedback, discuss the interaction and ask questions. This is a two-way conversation. The participant can ask, “Why did you respond that way when I asked about your sleep?” This is a powerful learning moment, because it allows the participant to see the interaction from the patient’s perspective.<br>
<br>
Afterwards, the participant returns to the facilitator for 5 to 10 min of debrief procedures. During this time, participants are provided the opportunity to speak with a psychologist for further debriefing if needed. <br>
<br>
This is a safety net. The study deals with a heavy topic—suicide—and the researchers are ethically obligated to ensure that participants are not emotionally distressed by the experience. The debrief is a chance to process the experience, and the offer of a psychologist is a proactive measure to support the participant’s well-being.<br>
<br>
All interactions are recorded, securely stored and subsequently coded for observed suicide prevention skills. <br>
<br>
This is where the data comes from. The recordings are not just for the participants’ benefit; they are the raw material for the study’s analysis. “Coding” means that trained raters will watch the videos and systematically score the participants’ performance against a predefined set of criteria. This is how the researchers can objectively measure whether the training worked.<br>
<br>
A portion (at least 20%) of recorded interactions are double coded for inter-rater reliability. <br>
<br>
Why double code? Because coding is subjective. Two different raters might see the same interaction and score it differently. By having two raters independently code the same video, the researchers can measure how much agreement there is. If they agree highly, they can be confident that the coding scheme is reliable and that the results are not just a product of one person’s opinion.<br>
<br>
Consensus meetings are conducted and discrepancies are resolved through discussion. When the two raters disagree, they don’t just flip a coin. They meet, discuss their reasoning, and come to a consensus. This is a quality-control step that ensures the final data is as accurate and fair as possible.<br>
<br>
Additionally, the videos are coded for SPs fidelity to the scenario and feedback procedures. <br>
<br>
This is a check on the SPs themselves. The researchers need to make sure that the SPs are consistently playing their role and delivering feedback in the same way. If one SP is more aggressive or more lenient, it could skew the results. This fidelity check ensures that the SPs are performing their roles as intended.<br>
<br>
To avoid drift in standardized procedures, SPs receive written feedback on fidelity to the character and feedback delivery. <br>
<br>
“Drift” is a subtle but real problem. Over time, even the best SPs might start to improvise or change their performance. The written feedback is a corrective measure, a reminder of the standards they need to maintain.<br>
<br>
After the SP test case at approximately 6-month follow-up, all participants complete a final survey including: knowledge and self-efficacy measures, retrospective self-report of transfer of training, intentions to use the suicide prevention training in future practice, self-reported use of suicide prevention skills since the online teaching program, and satisfaction with various aspects of the training.<br>
<br>
This is the long-term measurement. The researchers aren’t just interested in whether the training works immediately; they want to know if it sticks. The 6-month follow-up is a critical time point to see if the skills have been retained and, more importantly, if they have been applied in real clinical practice. The “retrospective self-report of transfer of training” is a clever way to ask, “Looking back, do you think you used what you learned?” This is a measure of the training’s real-world impact.<br>
<br>
Measures<br>
<br>
Now we move from the study design to the specific tools used to measure the outcomes. This is the “how” of the data collection.<br>
<br>
Self-report measures<br>
<br>
Demographics: All participants complete a survey of individual characteristics and demographics, including training program (residency, NP), gender, age, previous suicide prevention training, previous experience with SP procedures, and prior experience (professionally and personally) with suicide. <br>
<br>
Why collect all this? Because these are potential “confounding variables.” For example, if a participant has had a personal experience with suicide, they might be more sensitive to the topic and perform differently on the assessment. By collecting this data, the researchers can statistically control for these differences, or they can check to see if their groups are balanced on these characteristics.<br>
<br>
These data will be examined to determine any differences between those who consent for study participation and those who do not, and be used for analytic purposes. This is a check on selection bias. If the people who agree to participate are different from those who don’t, the results might not be generalizable to the entire population of trainees.<br>
<br>
Knowledge of suicide and suicide prevention: Participants complete a 17-item multiple choice knowledge assessment before and after viewing online teaching videos and again at 6-month follow-up. <br>
<br>
This is a classic pre-test/post-test design. By measuring knowledge at three time points, the researchers can see the immediate effect of the training (comparing pre- and post-video scores) and the long-term retention (comparing post-video and 6-month scores).<br>
<br>
The measure assesses knowledge of suicide risk assessment and management as covered in the online teaching program (e.g., “Which of the following best describes risk for suicide?”). <br>
<br>
This is a direct test of the content taught in the videos. The question is designed to be a straightforward check of factual recall.<br>
<br>
A measure of knowledge has been used in published assessments of the CTL training curriculum [27] with good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = .81–.87) and sensitivity to change (Cohen’s d = .90–1.35). <br>
<br>
Let’s decode these statistics. Cronbach’s α is a measure of internal consistency—how well the items on the test hang together. A value above .80 is generally considered good. Cohen’s d is a measure of effect size—how much the scores change. A d of .90–1.35 is a very large effect, meaning the training has a substantial impact on knowledge. This tells us that the measure is a reliable and sensitive tool.<br>
<br>
For the purposes of the current study, modifications will be made to the measure. This is a common practice. The researchers are adapting a validated tool to fit their specific context, which is more rigorous than creating a new measure from scratch.<br>
<br>
Suicide prevention self-efficacy: Participants complete a 20-item measure of self-efficacy at pre- and post-online teaching and again at 6-month follow-up. <br>
<br>
Self-efficacy is a person’s belief in their own ability to perform a task. It’s a powerful predictor of whether they will actually do the task in the real world. This measure is assessing the participants’ confidence in their clinical skills.<br>
<br>
The measure uses an 8-point Likert scale (ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”) to assess trainees’ self-efficacy regarding suicide risk assessment, treatment, and documentation (e.g., “I feel confident that I can ask directly about suicide,” “I feel confident in my knowledge about the key elements of a safety plan.”). <br>
<br>
The Likert scale is a common survey format. The 8-point scale is a bit unusual—many scales use 5 or 7 points—but it forces the respondent to take a side, as there is no neutral “middle” option. The examples given are excellent: they are specific, actionable skills, not vague statements.<br>
<br>
The original CTL self-efficacy measure [27] has acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = .95–.96) and sufficient sensitivity to change over time (Cohen’s d = 1.05–1.15); modifications will be made for the current study. <br>
<br>
Again, we see the same pattern: a validated measure with excellent psychometric properties, which will be adapted for this study.<br>
<br>

The core task for the participant is clear: assess the patient’s risk for suicidality and provide appropriate interventions—all within a strict 30-minute window. A timer is used to signal the end of the appointment, simulating the time pressures often present in real-world clinical environments. This time limit isn’t arbitrary; it’s designed to train clinicians to gather critical information efficiently and make sound clinical judgments under realistic constraints.<br>
<br>
After the interaction, the participant leaves the room for about 10 minutes. During this time, the SP—highly trained in both acting and clinical feedback—prepares personalized, immediate feedback. This isn’t generic praise or criticism; it’s structured and targeted. The SP uses a 15-item “feedback matrix” template, which serves as a checklist of key competencies related to suicide risk assessment and communication skills. For example, did the participant ask directly about suicidal thoughts? Did they respond with empathy when the patient expressed distress? Each item on the matrix helps the SP identify specific strengths and areas for improvement.<br>
<br>
When the participant returns, they spend 8 minutes receiving this feedback directly from the SP. This is a powerful learning moment. Because the SP has just experienced the interaction from the patient’s perspective, they can offer unique insights: not just *what* the participant did, but *how it felt* to receive it. The feedback includes clear explanations and the rationale behind each skill—why, for instance, directly asking about suicide doesn’t increase risk but actually builds trust. Participants are encouraged to ask questions, reflect on their performance, and discuss alternative approaches.<br>
<br>
Following the feedback session, the participant meets with a facilitator for a 5- to 10-minute debrief. This step ensures psychological safety. While the SP provides clinical and communication feedback, the facilitator offers a supportive space to process the emotional weight of the interaction. Participants are given the opportunity to speak with a psychologist during this time if they feel distressed—a crucial safeguard, especially when dealing with emotionally intense scenarios like suicide risk assessment.<br>
<br>
All interactions are recorded and stored securely. Later, trained coders review the videos to objectively assess the participant’s use of suicide prevention skills. This isn’t just about checking boxes; it’s about measuring real behavioral change. To ensure accuracy and consistency in coding, at least 20% of the videos are double-coded by independent raters. If there’s a disagreement between coders, a consensus meeting is held to resolve the discrepancy through discussion. This process strengthens the reliability of the data and ensures that the evaluation of skills is as fair and objective as possible.<br>
<br>
In addition to assessing participants, the videos are also used to monitor the SPs themselves. Coders evaluate how faithfully each SP adhered to the scenario script and feedback procedures. This quality control step is vital—over time, even well-trained SPs might unintentionally drift from the intended portrayal or feedback style. To prevent this, SPs receive written feedback on their performance, helping them stay aligned with the training standards. It’s a feedback loop for the feedback providers, ensuring consistency across all interactions.<br>
<br>
After the SP test case—typically around the 6-month follow-up—participants complete a final survey. This survey captures several important outcomes: their retained knowledge and self-efficacy, their retrospective self-report of whether they’ve transferred the training to real practice, their intentions to use these skills in the future, their actual self-reported use of suicide prevention techniques since the training, and their overall satisfaction with different aspects of the program. This long-term follow-up helps answer the critical question: Did this training make a lasting difference in how clinicians approach suicide prevention?<br>

### Measures<br>
<br>
#### Self-report measures<br>
<br>
**Demographics**  <br>
All participants complete a survey collecting basic individual characteristics and demographic information. This includes their training program (e.g., residency or nurse practitioner), gender, age, prior suicide prevention training, previous experience with SP encounters, and any professional or personal history with suicide. Why collect this? First, researchers need to know if the people who choose to join the study differ in meaningful ways from those who don’t—this helps assess potential selection bias. Second, these variables might influence how participants respond to training, so they’re included in analyses to better understand the results. For example, someone with prior suicide training might show less improvement simply because they started at a higher level.<br>
<br>
**Knowledge of suicide and suicide prevention**  <br>
Participants take a 17-item multiple-choice assessment before and after viewing the online teaching videos, and again at the 6-month follow-up. This test measures their understanding of key concepts in suicide risk assessment and management—exactly the content covered in the training. One sample question might be: “Which of the following best describes risk for suicide?” The goal is to see not just whether people *remember* facts, but whether they’ve internalized core principles that guide clinical decision-making.<br>
<br>
This knowledge measure isn’t new—it’s been used in previous evaluations of the CTL (Columbia Training in Leadership) curriculum and has strong psychometric properties. That means it’s both reliable (Cronbach’s α = .81–.87, indicating good internal consistency—the items hang together well) and sensitive to change (Cohen’s d = .90–1.35, a large effect size showing it can detect real learning). For this study, some modifications will be made to better align the questions with the updated training content, ensuring the test remains accurate and relevant.<br>
<br>
**Suicide prevention self-efficacy**  <br>
Self-efficacy is a powerful psychological concept—it’s not about what you *can* do, but what you *believe* you can do. Participants complete a 20-item scale at pre-training, post-training, and 6-month follow-up to measure their confidence in performing suicide prevention tasks. The scale uses an 8-point Likert format, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” allowing for nuanced responses.<br>
<br>
Sample items include: “I feel confident that I can ask directly about suicide,” and “I feel confident in my knowledge about the key elements of a safety plan.” These aren’t abstract statements—they reflect real clinical behaviors that can save lives. The original CTL self-efficacy measure has demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = .95–.96) and strong sensitivity to change over time (Cohen’s d = 1.05–1.15), meaning it effectively captures growth in confidence as a result of training. For this study, the measure will be adapted slightly to ensure it fits the current program’s goals and content.<br>
<br>
**Transfer of training**  <br>
Finally, researchers want to know: Will participants actually *use* what they’ve learned in real clinical settings? To assess this, they use an 8-item measure adapted from three subscales of the *Learning Transfer System Inventory* [28]: transfer design, perceived content validity, and opportunity to use.</div></td>
