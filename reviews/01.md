<td valign="top"><div>## Viral Susceptibility and Transcriptomic Response in *C. briggsae*<br>
<br>
Since the reference strain AF16 of *Caenorhabditis briggsae* does not support replication of the Santeuil virus under our experimental conditions, we turned to a wild isolate—JU1264—which we had previously shown to be susceptible to Santeuil virus infection [18]. This switch was essential to study the host response to the virus in a biologically relevant context. Using JU1264, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to identify changes in gene expression following Santeuil virus infection.<br>
<br>
We identified 258 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in *C. briggsae* after Santeuil virus infection (using edgeR, with n = 3 biological replicates, and a false discovery rate [FDR] &lt; 0.05; see Additional file 2). Of these, the vast majority—221 genes—were up-regulated, meaning their expression increased in response to the virus. Only 37 genes were down-regulated, indicating reduced expression. This strong bias toward up-regulation suggests that the host is mounting an active transcriptional response, likely involving defense mechanisms or stress pathways.<br>
<br>
Now, a quick but important technical note: when analyzing the RNA-seq data from JU1264, we mapped the sequence reads to the AF16 reference transcriptome. Why? Because AF16 is the best-annotated *C. briggsae* reference genome, even though JU1264 is a different wild strain. While AF16 and JU1264 are closely related, they still have genetic differences. Some sequence variants in JU1264 may not align perfectly to the AF16 reference, especially in regions with high divergence. This means that reads from certain genes in JU1264 might not map at all or might map poorly, leading to incomplete detection of expression changes. As a result, our list of DEGs could be an underestimate—some real changes in gene expression might have been missed due to this mapping limitation. It’s a common challenge in comparative genomics when working with non-reference isolates.<br>
<br>
To validate our RNA-seq findings, we used an independent method: quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). This technique allows us to measure the expression levels of specific genes with high sensitivity and accuracy. We selected two up-regulated genes for validation:<br>
<br>
- **CBG03198**: This gene has orthologs (evolutionarily related genes) in *C. elegans* that were also up-regulated during viral infection, suggesting a potentially conserved role in antiviral defense.<br>
- **CBG06596**: This is an ortholog of the *C17H1* gene family in *C. briggsae*, a group we’ll see again as a recurring player in antiviral responses.<br>
<br>
The qRT-PCR results from an independent infection experiment matched the RNA-seq data closely—both genes showed increased expression, confirming the reliability of our transcriptomic profiling (see Additional file 6). This cross-validation strengthens our confidence that the observed changes are real and reproducible.<br>
<br>
Now, let’s zoom out and ask a bigger question: Are the host responses to viral infection similar across related species? To explore this, we compared the transcriptional response in *C. briggsae* to what we know from its well-studied cousin, *Caenorhabditis elegans*. About 60% of genes in *C. briggsae* have clear orthologs in *C. elegans* [34], making such comparisons meaningful.<br>
<br>
We focused on the set of 320 genes in *C. elegans* that were differentially expressed upon Orsay virus infection—either in the wild-type N2 strain, the *rde-1* mutant strain, or both (this is called the &quot;union&quot; of DEGs from both conditions). From this list, 197 genes have identifiable orthologs in *C. briggsae*. Among these 197, we found that **59** had orthologs in *C. briggsae* that were also differentially expressed after Santeuil virus infection (see Fig. 3 and Additional file 7). This overlap suggests a core, evolutionarily conserved transcriptional response to viral infection.<br>
<br>
Let’s unpack that number: of these 59 shared DEGs, **57 were up-regulated in both species**. This striking consistency implies that many aspects of the antiviral response have been preserved through evolution. Only two genes showed divergent regulation:<br>
<br>
- **hmit-1.1**: This gene was *repressed* (down-regulated) in both contexts—during Orsay virus infection in *C. elegans* (*rde-1* background) and during Santeuil virus infection in *C. briggsae* (JU1264). Its consistent down-regulation might indicate a role that viruses exploit, or perhaps it’s part of a metabolic shift during infection.<br>
- **clec-7**: This gene showed opposite responses. It was repressed during Orsay virus infection in *C. elegans* but *induced* (up-regulated) during Santeuil virus infection in *C. briggsae*. This kind of divergence can happen when genes evolve new regulatory roles in different species or in response to different viruses.<br>
<br>
In total, we define **58 genes** as part of a **conserved transcriptional response to viral infection**—those that are differentially expressed in both species in the same direction (mostly up-regulated). Let’s look more closely at the subgroups within this conserved set.<br>
<br>
First, there are **29 genes** in *C. elegans* that were induced (up-regulated) in *both* N2 and *rde-1* mutants upon Orsay virus infection—meaning their induction doesn’t depend on the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway, since *rde-1* is essential for RNAi. These 29 genes have orthologs in *C. briggsae* that were also up-regulated after Santeuil infection. This independence from RNAi suggests these genes are part of a broader, perhaps more ancient, antiviral defense system.<br>
<br>
Among these 29 are several notable gene families and individual genes:<br>
<br>
- **14 members of the C17H1 family**: These are highlighted in our data as strongly induced across conditions. We’ll see shortly that they are enriched in functional analyses, suggesting a central role in antiviral defense.<br>
- **Four genes with DUF713 domains**: DUF stands for &quot;Domain of Unknown Function,&quot; but their consistent up-regulation hints at an important, yet still mysterious, role in infection response.<br>
- **C04F12.1**: This gene is part of the RNAi pathway itself, which is fascinating—it’s induced even in an *rde-1* mutant, suggesting feedback or compensatory regulation within the pathway.<br>
- **dod-23**: This gene lies downstream of *daf-16*, a key transcription factor in stress response and longevity. Its induction links viral infection to broader stress and survival pathways.<br>
<br>
The remaining 9 of these 29 genes currently have no known function—black boxes in our understanding. Their conservation and strong induction make them compelling candidates for future study; they might represent novel antiviral effectors.<br>
<br>
Next, we found **2 genes** that were induced in *C. elegans* N2 (wild-type) but *not* in the *rde-1* mutant—meaning their induction depends on a functional RNAi pathway. Their orthologs in *C. briggsae* were also differentially expressed after Santeuil infection. These are:<br>
<br>
- **F14F9.3**: A gene containing a zinc finger domain of the C6HC type. Zinc finger domains often bind RNA or DNA and are involved in gene regulation. Their involvement here suggests a role in orchestrating the transcriptional response.<br>
- **ZK177.8**: This gene has a human ortholog called **SAMHD1**, which is a well-known antiviral protein that inhibits replication of HIV-1 by depleting cellular nucleotide pools [35]. Finding this gene in our list is exciting—it suggests that even in nematodes, some antiviral mechanisms are shared with mammals, pointing to deeply conserved defense strategies.<br>
<br>
Finally, there were **27 genes** in *C. elegans* that were differentially expressed only in the *rde-1* mutant (not in N2), and whose orthologs in *C. briggsae* were also DEGs. This set may represent genes that are normally suppressed by the RNAi pathway or are part of a compensatory response when RNAi is defective. Among these:<br>
<br>
- **6 are immune-related genes**<br>
- **4 contain zinc finger (C6HC-type) domains**<br>
<br>
These findings suggest that when the primary RNAi defense is compromised, the host activates alternative immune mechanisms, some of which are conserved in *C. briggsae*.<br>
