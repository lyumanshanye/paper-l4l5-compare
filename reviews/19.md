<br>
Before we interpret these curves, let&#x27;s recall what we are looking at. The effective potential \( V(\rho) \) tells us the energy of the system as a function of the &quot;condensate&quot; field \( \rho \). The ground state of the system—the state of lowest energy—corresponds to the minimum of this potential. If the minimum occurs at \( \rho = 0 \), the symmetry is unbroken. If the minimum occurs at a non-zero value of \( \rho \), the symmetry is spontaneously broken, and the fermions and scalars acquire a dynamical mass proportional to that value of \( \rho \).<br>
<br>
Now, let&#x27;s examine the three curves. For \( f(\xi_1,\xi_2)_{\text{RG}} = 0 \), the potential is a simple quadratic function that has its minimum exactly at \( \rho = 0 \). For \( f(\xi_1,\xi_2)_{\text{RG}} = 10 \), the potential is non-negative and also has its minimum at \( \rho = 0 \). However, for \( f(\xi_1,\xi_2)_{\text{RG}} = -10 \), the potential develops a minimum at a non-zero value of \( \rho \), indicating that the chiral symmetry is broken down.<br>
<br>

\]<br>

\]<br>

This function is negative for all positive \( x \), which is a property we will use shortly. The constant \( \gamma \) is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.<br>

Let’s walk through what’s happening here step by step. We’re studying how quantum fields behave in curved spacetime, specifically within a model called the supersymmetric Nambu–Jona-Lasinio (SUSY NJL) model. One of the key tools we use is the *effective potential*, V(ρ), which tells us about the possible stable states (or &quot;phases&quot;) of the system. The variable ρ is related to the expectation value of a composite field—it will eventually tell us whether fermions and scalars in the theory acquire a dynamical mass.<br>
<br>
But before we get to the full expression for V(ρ), we encounter a mathematical challenge: an integral that blows up (diverges) when the integration variable t approaches zero. This is common in quantum field theory—it signals that we’re probing physics at extremely short distances (or high energies), where our current description might break down.<br>
<br>
To handle this, we introduce a *proper time cutoff*, Λ. Think of this like putting a lower limit on how finely we can probe spacetime—similar to how a microscope has a resolution limit. We’re saying: “We won’t consider physics at scales smaller than 1/Λ.” This makes the integral finite and physically meaningful.<br>
<br>
So instead of integrating t from 0 to ∞ (which would give infinity), we start the integral at t = 1/Λ². That’s what the next expression does:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
I \rightarrow \frac{R}{2(4\pi)^2} \int_{1/\Lambda^2}^{\infty} dt\, \frac{1}{t^2} \left( e^{-t\rho^2} - 1 \right)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Now, this integral might look intimidating, but let’s break it down. We’re integrating a function that combines an exponential decay (e⁻ᵗρ²) and a constant (−1), scaled by 1/t². The exponential term dies off at large t, and the subtraction of 1 ensures that the integrand behaves nicely at large t. But near t = 0 (or rather, near t = 1/Λ² due to the cutoff), the 1/t² factor makes the integral sensitive to high-energy effects.<br>
<br>
After performing this integral (which involves some clever calculus and special functions), we arrive at a closed-form expression:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
I = \frac{R}{2(4\pi)^2} \left[ \rho^2 \, \text{Ei}\left( -\frac{\rho^2}{\Lambda^2} \right) + \Lambda^2 \left( e^{-\rho^2/\Lambda^2} - 1 \right) \right], \tag{13}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Here, **Ei(−x)** is the *exponential integral function*, a special function that often appears in physics when integrating exponentials over infinite ranges. It’s not an elementary function like sine or log, but it’s well-studied and tabulated.<br>
<br>
Let’s pause to understand Ei(−x). It’s defined as:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\text{Ei}(-x) = -\int_x^\infty dt\, \frac{e^{-t}}{t}, \quad (x &gt; 0). \tag{14}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
This means we’re integrating the function e⁻ᵗ/t from x to infinity. Because of the 1/t factor, the integral diverges logarithmically as t → 0, but since we start at x &gt; 0, it’s finite. In fact, for small x, Ei(−x) behaves like ln(x) plus some constants. The full expansion is:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\text{Ei}(-x) = \ln x + \gamma + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-x)^n}{n \cdot n!} &lt; 0, \quad (x &gt; 0),<br>
$$<br>
<br>
where γ is the Euler-Mascheroni constant (approximately 0.5772). Note that Ei(−x) is always *negative* for positive x—this will be important later when we analyze the shape of the potential.<br>
<br>
Now, plugging this result back into our effective action, we obtain the full effective potential in the leading order of the 1/N_c expansion (a large-N approximation commonly used to make strongly interacting theories tractable):<br>
<br>
$$<br>
V(\rho) = \frac{1}{G}\rho^2 - \frac{R}{(4\pi)^2} f(\xi_1,\xi_2) \left[ \rho^2 \, \text{Ei}\left( -\frac{\rho^2}{\Lambda^2} \right) + \Lambda^2 \left( e^{-\rho^2/\Lambda^2} - 1 \right) \right], \tag{15}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Here, G is a coupling constant that governs the strength of the interaction, R is the Ricci scalar (a measure of spacetime curvature), and f(ξ₁,ξ₂) is a combination of nonminimal coupling parameters ξ₁ and ξ₂ that describe how the scalar and fermion fields couple to gravity. Specifically:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
f(\xi_1,\xi_2) = \frac{1}{2} - \xi_1 - \xi_2. \tag{16}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
This function f(ξ₁,ξ₂) acts like a *control knob* that determines how curvature affects symmetry breaking. By adjusting ξ₁ and ξ₂ (the ways fields interact with gravity), we can change the sign and magnitude of f, which in turn changes the behavior of the potential.<br>
<br>
The physical state of the system corresponds to the *minimum* of this effective potential V(ρ)—just like a ball rolling to the bottom of a valley. So to find out whether particles acquire mass or whether symmetries are broken, we need to study how V(ρ) behaves for different values of f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG.<br>
<br>
In Figure 1, we plot GV(ρ)/Λ² versus ρ/Λ for three representative values: f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG = 10, 0, and −10. These curves reveal dramatically different physical scenarios.<br>
<br>
Let’s consider each case.<br>
<br>
**Case 1: f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG = 0 (Flat spacetime or balanced couplings)**<br>
<br>
When f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG = 0, the curvature-dependent term in V(ρ) vanishes, regardless of whether R is zero or not (though typically this case corresponds to flat spacetime, R = 0). Then the potential reduces to a simple quadratic:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
V(\rho) = \frac{1}{G} \rho^2.<br>
$$<br>
<br>
This is a parabola opening upwards. Its minimum is clearly at ρ = 0. This means no dynamical mass is generated—the chiral symmetry of the theory remains unbroken. All particles stay massless. This is shown in Figure 1 as the upward-opening curve centered at the origin.<br>
<br>
**Case 2: f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG &gt; 0 (e.g., f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG = 10)**<br>
<br>
Now the curvature term is active and multiplied by a positive f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG. Recall from Eq. (14) that Ei(−ρ²/Λ²) is negative, and the combination in brackets in Eq. (15) is such that the entire curvature correction term becomes positive when multiplied by −f(ξ₁,ξ₂) (since there’s a minus sign in front).<br>
<br>
So the full potential remains *non-negative* and still minimized at ρ = 0. The potential may be slightly modified in shape, but the minimum doesn’t shift. Again, no symmetry breaking occurs. This is illustrated in Figure 1 by the curve for f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG = 10—it’s still steepest at ρ = 0.<br>
<br>
**Case 3: f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG &lt; 0 (e.g., f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG = −10)**<br>
<br>
Now things get interesting. When f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG is negative, the sign of the curvature term flips. The correction to the potential now works *against* the positive ρ²/G term. Because Ei(−ρ²/Λ²) is negative, multiplying it by a negative f(ξ₁,ξ₂) makes the whole term positive in a way that can create a *dip* away from ρ = 0.<br>
<br>
Looking at Figure 1, we see that for f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG = −10, the potential has a minimum at some ρ &gt; 0. This means the system prefers a state where ρ is nonzero—this is *dynamical symmetry breaking*. The chiral symmetry is spontaneously broken, and fermions ψ and scalars φ acquire a dynamical mass.<br>
<br>
The value of ρ at the minimum gives us the mass: m = ⟨ρ⟩.<br>
<br>
To find this minimum precisely, we take the derivative of V(ρ) with respect to ρ and set it to zero. This is the *stationary condition*:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\frac{\partial V(\rho)}{\partial \rho} = \rho \left[ \frac{2}{G} - \frac{2R}{(4\pi)^2} f(\xi_1,\xi_2) \, \text{Ei}\left( -\frac{\rho^2}{\Lambda^2} \right) \right] = 0. \tag{17}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
This equation has two possible solutions:<br>
<br>
1. ρ = 0 — the symmetric phase.<br>
2. The term in brackets is zero — the broken phase.<br>
<br>
For a nontrivial solution (ρ ≠ 0), we require:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\frac{2}{G} = \frac{2R}{(4\pi)^2} f(\xi_1,\xi_2) \, \text{Ei}\left( -\frac{\rho^2}{\Lambda^2} \right)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Solving for the coupling, we get:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\frac{16\pi^2}{RG} = f(\xi_1,\xi_2) \, \text{Ei}\left( -\frac{m^2}{\Lambda^2} \right), \tag{18}<br>
$$<br>
<br>
where m = ⟨ρ⟩ is the dynamically generated mass.<br>
<br>
Note that Ei(−m²/Λ²) &lt; 0, as established earlier. So for this equation to hold, the right-hand side must be positive (since the left-hand side 16π²/(RG) is typically positive for reasonable G and R). This only happens if f(ξ₁,ξ₂) is *negative*—because a negative times a negative gives a positive.<br>
<br>
This explains why symmetry breaking only occurs when f(ξ₁,ξ₂)RG &lt; 0: only then can the equation support a solution with m &gt; 0.<br>
