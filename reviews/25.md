Before we move on, let’s pause to make sure the key player here is clear. **Nrf2** (short for Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) is a master regulator of the cell’s antioxidant defense. Think of it as a fire alarm system: when cells are stressed by something like UV light, Nrf2 should rush into the nucleus—the cell’s command center—and switch on a battery of protective genes. In skin fibroblasts (a type of connective tissue cell), UV A does trigger this alarm loudly. But in keratinocytes (the cells that make up the bulk of our outer skin layer), the alarm barely whispers. The data above show that even at a hefty dose of 400 kJ·m⁻², Nrf2 levels in HaCaT cells (a standard keratinocyte cell line) only creep up by less than 2-fold, and the protein stays mostly put in the nucleus even without any irradiation. This is a crucial clue: keratinocytes may not rely on Nrf2 as their primary defense against UV A, or they may use a different, slower pathway. Keep this in mind, because it sets the stage for the next experiment, where we ask: if we remove Nrf2 entirely, does the cell become more vulnerable?<br>
<br>
3.3. Reduction of Nrf2 increased cell damage induced by 400 kJ·m⁻² of UVA in HaCaT cells<br>
<br>
A study demonstrated that a moderate dose (250 kJ·m⁻²) of UV A irradiation significantly induced the expression of Nrf2 in skin fibroblasts and that Nrf2 protected the cells against this dose of UV A irradiation-mediated membrane damage, which was measured with an LDH assay (10). Nrf2 was expected to protect HaCaT skin keratinocytes, though UV A irradiation caused only a slight increase in Nrf2 protein levels and nuclear accumulation in these cells (Figure 2). To demonstrate the importance of Nrf2 in UV A irradiation-mediated cell damage, the Nrf2 gene was knocked down in HaCaT cells using siRNA. Knock down of Nrf2 protein levels was confirmed by Western blotting analysis, which revealed a significant decrease in Nrf2 protein levels following siRNA transfection (Figure 3).<br>
<br>
Let’s unpack this strategy. The researchers are essentially asking: *If Nrf2 is only weakly activated, does it still matter?* To answer this, they use a powerful tool called **siRNA** (small interfering RNA). This is like sending a targeted “mute” signal to a specific gene—here, the Nrf2 gene—so that the cell can’t produce the Nrf2 protein. The Western blot in Figure 3 confirms that the mute worked: Nrf2 levels dropped dramatically. Now, with Nrf2 out of the picture, the researchers can compare what happens to cells with and without this protective protein when they’re hit with UV A.<br>
<br>
Cell damage was demonstrated by the loss of the integrity of the membrane, as gauged by LDH release and viability (MTT assay). HaCaT Cells were pretreated with vehicle or scrambled siRNA and siNrf2 during plating and grown to 90% confluence. Cells were then either sham or UV A (400 kJ·m⁻²)-irradiated and further incubated in normal medium for 4 h. <br>
Let’s break down what these numbers mean. **LDH** (lactate dehydrogenase) is an enzyme that normally lives inside the cell. If the cell membrane gets damaged—like a leaky boat—LDH spills out into the surrounding fluid. So measuring LDH in the culture medium is like checking for water outside a submarine: the more LDH outside, the more holes in the membrane. **MTT assay** is a different readout: it measures how many cells are still alive and metabolically active. Think of it as a headcount of healthy cells.<br>
<br>
Here’s the story the data tell. Without UV, silencing Nrf2 does almost nothing—cells are fine, membranes intact. But when you hit the cells with UV, the damage is real: LDH leakage jumps 1.7-fold, and viability drops by 10%. Now, if you also remove Nrf2 (with siNrf2), the damage gets worse. LDH leakage climbs to 2.3-fold (with 10 nM siNrf2) and 2.5-fold (with 50 nM siNrf2). Viability drops further, to 15% and 20% loss, respectively. The fact that a higher dose of siNrf2 causes more damage suggests a dose-dependent effect—more Nrf2 loss, more vulnerability. And the fact that a second, independent siNrf2 (targeting a different part of the gene) gives the same result rules out off-target effects. So even though UV A only weakly activates Nrf2 in keratinocytes, Nrf2 still plays a meaningful protective role. It’s not the star of the show, but it’s a crucial supporting actor.<br>

<br>
UVA irradiation is known to strongly activate the Nrf2 pathway in FEK4 human skin fibroblasts—cells that provide structural support in the skin—but only weakly activates Nrf2 in human skin keratinocytes, which are the primary cell type in the outermost layer of the skin (10,14,15). This difference is important because Nrf2 is a key regulatory protein that helps protect cells from oxidative stress, such as that caused by UV radiation. When activated, Nrf2 moves into the nucleus and turns on a network of antioxidant and detoxifying genes, essentially putting the cell into defense mode.<br>
<br>
Given this, researchers wanted to explore whether a moderate to high dose of UVA radiation—specifically 400 kJ·m⁻²—could still influence Nrf2 activity in keratinocytes, even if previous studies suggested only weak activation. To test this, they used HaCaT cells, a well-established model of human skin keratinocytes, and exposed them to this high dose of UVA. They then measured Nrf2 protein levels at 4 and 8 hours after irradiation.<br>
<br>
The results, shown in Figure 2A, revealed only a modest increase—less than two-fold—in Nrf2 protein levels after irradiation. That’s a relatively small change, especially when compared to the robust activation seen in fibroblasts under similar conditions. But protein levels aren’t the whole story. Where the protein is located in the cell matters just as much. So the researchers also performed immunostaining to visualize where Nrf2 was located within the HaCaT cells.<br>
<br>
Here’s what they found: even in the *non-irradiated* control cells, Nrf2 was already present in the nucleus. This is unusual because in most cell types, Nrf2 is normally held in the cytoplasm and only moves into the nucleus when activated by stress. The fact that it’s already nuclear in unstimulated HaCaT cells suggests that these keratinocytes may have a baseline level of Nrf2 activity, possibly as a preemptive defense mechanism given their frontline position in the skin.<br>
<br>
After UVA irradiation, there was only a slight additional accumulation of Nrf2 in the nucleus at 4 and 8 hours. While this hints at some level of activation, it’s not a dramatic response. Taken together, these findings indicate that despite Nrf2 being a powerful protector in skin fibroblasts when activated by UVA (as shown in earlier studies (10,14)), it does not become significantly activated in human skin keratinocytes—even under a relatively high dose of UVA radiation. This raises an important question: if Nrf2 isn’t strongly turned on in keratinocytes after UV exposure, does it still play a meaningful protective role in these cells?<br>
<br>
### 3.3. Reduction of Nrf2 increased cell damage induced by 400 kJ·m⁻² of UVA in HaCaT cells<br>
<br>
We know from prior research that in skin fibroblasts, a moderate dose of UVA (250 kJ·m⁻²) significantly boosts Nrf2 expression, and this increase helps protect the cells from membrane damage caused by oxidative stress. That damage is typically measured using the LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) assay, which detects when cell membranes become leaky—a clear sign of injury.<br>
<br>
So, even though UVA only causes a small rise in Nrf2 levels and nuclear presence in HaCaT keratinocytes, could Nrf2 still be playing a subtle but important protective role? To answer this, the researchers used a powerful technique called gene knockdown. Specifically, they used small interfering RNA (siRNA) to reduce the expression of the Nrf2 gene in HaCaT cells. This approach allows scientists to &quot;turn down&quot; a specific protein and observe what happens when it’s missing—like removing a single player from a sports team to see how the team performs.<br>
<br>
After transfecting the cells with siRNA targeting Nrf2 (siNrf2), they confirmed the knockdown using Western blotting, a method that detects protein levels. The results (Figure 3) showed a clear and significant reduction in Nrf2 protein, meaning the intervention worked as intended.<br>
<br>
Now came the critical test: how would these Nrf2-deficient cells respond to UVA radiation? To assess cell damage, the researchers looked at two key indicators: membrane integrity and cell viability.<br>
<br>
- **Membrane integrity** was measured by the LDH assay: when cells are damaged, LDH leaks out into the surrounding medium. More LDH release = more damage.<br>
- **Cell viability** was assessed using the MTT assay, which measures the metabolic activity of living cells. A drop in MTT signal means fewer healthy, functioning cells.<br>
<br>
The experimental setup was carefully designed:<br>
- HaCaT cells were pretreated with either a control (scrambled) siRNA or Nrf2-targeting siRNA (siNrf2) at two concentrations: 10 nM and 50 nM.<br>
- Cells were grown to 90% confluence (almost a full layer) to mimic natural tissue density.<br>
- Then, they were either not irradiated (sham) or exposed to 400 kJ·m⁻² of UVA.<br>
- After irradiation, cells were incubated for 4 more hours in normal growth medium to allow damage responses to develop.<br>
<br>
Let’s walk through the results step by step.<br>
<br>
In the absence of UVA (sham condition), neither the scrambled siRNA nor siNrf2 caused much LDH leakage or loss of viability (Figure 4A and 4B). This is reassuring—it means the siRNA treatment itself isn’t toxic. The cells are healthy and intact without radiation.<br>
<br>
But when cells were exposed to UVA:<br>
- Control cells (treated with scrambled siRNA) showed a **1.7-fold increase in LDH leakage** (Figure 4A), indicating significant membrane damage.<br>
- Their viability dropped by about **10%** (Figure 4B), which is a measurable but not catastrophic loss.<br>
<br>
Now, here’s where it gets interesting: when Nrf2 was knocked down before UVA exposure, the damage got noticeably worse.<br>
<br>
- With **10 nM siNrf2**, LDH leakage jumped to **2.3-fold** higher than control.<br>
- With **50 nM siNrf2**, it increased even further—to **2.5-fold** (p &lt; 0.05), meaning the difference is statistically significant.<br>
- Similarly, the loss of viability increased from 10% to **15% and 20%**, respectively, with the two doses of siNrf2 (Figure 4B).<br>
<br>
To make sure this wasn’t an off-target effect (i.e., the siRNA accidentally disrupting another gene), the researchers tested a second siRNA that targeted a different part (exon) of the Nrf2 gene. It produced the same result—increased damage—confirming that the effect was specifically due to reduced Nrf2.<br>
<br>
What does all this mean? Even though UVA doesn’t strongly *activate* Nrf2 in HaCaT keratinocytes, Nrf2 still plays a crucial protective role. Think of it like a smoke detector that’s already on standby. You don’t need to turn it on during a fire—it’s already active enough to help. In this case, even the baseline or slightly elevated level of Nrf2 is doing important work shielding the cells from UV-induced damage.<br>
<br>
So, while keratinocytes don’t mount a strong Nrf2 response to UVA like fibroblasts do, the Nrf2 they *do* have is functionally important. Removing it makes the cells significantly more vulnerable. This tells us that Nrf2 is a key defender in human skin keratinocytes, even in the absence of robust induction by UVA.<br>
<br>
These findings highlight a subtle but vital point in cellular defense mechanisms: sometimes, protection doesn’t come from a dramatic on-switch, but from a quiet, constant readiness. And disrupting that readiness—even slightly—can have real consequences.<br>
