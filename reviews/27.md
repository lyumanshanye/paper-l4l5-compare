When the electric field is switched on, the parabola representing the potential energy surface in the presence of the field is shifted downward relative to the other one. This shift lowers the height of the energy barrier that separates the two molecular states, making it easier for an electron to transfer in the direction favored by the field.<br>
<br>
The barrier height for forward transfer (E₊) and backward transfer (E₋) in the presence of a field E can be written as [5]:<br>
<br>
E± = λ/4 (1 ± eEa/λ)² = λ/4 ± eEa/2 + (eEa)²/(4λ)   (12)<br>
<br>
Let’s unpack this equation carefully. Here, λ is the reorganization energy—the energy cost to rearrange the nuclear positions (the atoms) when the electron moves. The term eEa represents the energy gained (or lost) by the electron due to the electric field, where e is the elementary charge and a is the distance between the two molecules. The plus sign corresponds to the forward direction (electron moving with the field), and the minus sign to the backward direction (electron moving against the field). The first term, λ/4, is the barrier height when no field is present. The second term, ±eEa/2, shows how the field tilts the energy landscape: it lowers the forward barrier and raises the backward one. The third term, (eEa)²/(4λ), is a small correction that becomes important only when the field is strong.<br>
<br>
The rate of electron transfer (ET) between the two molecules is given by:<br>
<br>
k± = νn κ · exp(−E± / (kBT))   (13)<br>
<br>
Here, νn is the frequency of the vibrational mode that is coupled to the electron transfer (we assume a single unique mode). T is the temperature, and kB is the Boltzmann constant, which converts energy into temperature units. The corresponding vibrational modes are what actually drive the electron transfer—they provide the nuclear motion that brings the system to the crossing point. In the case of metal complexes, the relevant mode is the breathing or half-breathing mode, which typically lies in the range of 500–600 cm⁻¹.<br>
<br>
Our goal now is to find the net rate of electron transfer, which is the difference between the forward and backward rates. This difference is what drives a net current. If the electric field E is small, the third term in (12) can be neglected, and we can perform a Taylor expansion of the exponentials. Doing so yields:<br>
<br>
k₊ − k₋ = νnκ [exp(−E₊/(kBT)) − exp(−E₋/(kBT))] ≈ νnκ · (eEa)/(kBT) · exp(−λ/(4kBT))   (14)<br>
<br>
This result tells us that the net rate is proportional to the field strength E, which makes sense: the stronger the field, the more the forward direction is favored. The exponential factor exp(−λ/(4kBT)) is the thermal activation factor—it shows that the net rate is suppressed by the energy barrier λ/4, which is the barrier height when no field is present.<br>
<br>
The quantity κ is called the electronic factor, and it is given by [3]:<br>
<br>
κ = 2 [1 − exp(−νel/(2νn))] / [2 − exp(−νel/(2νn))] ;   νel = Δ₁₂/(4πℏ) · (π³/(λkBT))^(1/2)   (15)<br>
<br>
Here, νel is called the “electronic frequency,” while νn is the nuclear frequency. The electronic frequency measures how fast the electron can tunnel between the two sites, while the nuclear frequency measures how fast the atoms vibrate. κ and νel will be derived in detail below.<br>
<br>
Now, combining equations (9), (11), and (14), we can express the total conductivity σ (which is the current density J divided by the electric field E) as:<br>
<br>
σ = J/E = νnκ · (ne²a²)/(kBT) · exp(−λ/(4kBT))   (16)<br>
<br>
provided that the coupling Δ is small. Here, n is the concentration of charge carriers, and e is the elementary charge.<br>
<br>
Let’s consider a special case: when the electronic frequency is much smaller than the nuclear frequency (νel &lt;&lt; νn). In this limit, we can approximate κ ≈ νel/νn from (15). Substituting this into (16), we find that the conductivity becomes proportional to the square of the coupling (Δ²) and is independent of the nuclear frequency νn:<br>
<br>
σ = J/E = (ne²a²)/(kBT) · (Δ²)/(4πℏ) · (π³/(λkBT))^(1/2) · exp(−λ/(4kBT))   (17)<br>

This is a remarkable result: the conductivity no longer depends on how fast the nuclei vibrate, but only on how strongly the electronic states are coupled. The result in (17) is shown in Fig. 5. Notice that the same equation is used both in the activated region up to T ≈ 200 K, where conductivity increases with temperature (because thermal energy helps overcome the barrier), and in the higher temperature region where the conductivity decreases with increasing temperature, just as in a metal.<br>

<td valign="top"><div>J Supercond Nov Magn (2017) 30:275–285 279  <br>
When an external electric field is applied, the energy landscape for electron transfer (ET) changes. Specifically, the parabolic potential energy curve associated with the final state—meaning the state the electron is moving into—is shifted downward relative to the initial state. This shift effectively lowers the energy barrier the electron must overcome to move forward, making electron transfer easier in the direction favored by the field.<br>
<br>
Let’s unpack what this means. Imagine two molecules sitting next to each other, and an electron wants to jump from one to the other. Without any external influence, there’s a certain energy &quot;hill&quot; the electron must climb—this is the activation barrier. But when you apply an electric field that pushes the electron in one direction, it’s like tilting the hill: one side becomes easier to climb, the other harder. The height of this barrier now depends on the direction of transfer—forward (with the field) or backward (against the field).<br>
<br>
The expressions for these modified barrier heights, $ E_+ $ (forward) and $ E_- $ (backward), in the presence of an electric field $ E $, are given by:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
E_\pm = \frac{\lambda}{4} \left(1 \pm \frac{eEa}{\lambda}\right)^2 = \frac{\lambda}{4} \pm \frac{eEa}{2} + \frac{(eEa)^2}{4\lambda} \quad (12)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Let’s break this down. Here:<br>
- $ \lambda $ is the **reorganization energy**—a key concept in electron transfer theory. Think of it as the energy cost required to rearrange the molecular structure and surrounding environment (like solvent molecules or lattice vibrations) when an electron moves. It’s like the “stiffness” of the system: a large $ \lambda $ means the environment resists change, making electron transfer harder.<br>
- $ e $ is the elementary charge,<br>
- $ a $ is the distance between the two molecules (or sites) involved in the transfer,<br>
- $ E $ is the applied electric field,<br>
- So $ eEa $ represents the **work done by the electric field** on the electron as it moves across the distance $ a $. This term directly lowers or raises the barrier depending on direction.<br>
<br>
Equation (12) shows how the barrier splits: increased in one direction, decreased in the other. The first term $ \lambda/4 $ is the intrinsic barrier when no field is present (this comes from Marcus theory). The second term $ \pm eEa/2 $ is the linear response to the field—this is what makes forward and backward transfers unequal. The third term, quadratic in $ E $, is usually small for weak fields and often neglected.<br>
<br>
Now, how fast does electron transfer actually happen? The rate of electron transfer in either direction is given by:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
k_\pm = \nu_n \kappa \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{E_\pm}{k_B T}\right) \quad (13)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
This is an Arrhenius-type expression: the rate depends exponentially on the barrier height divided by thermal energy $ k_B T $. But let’s look closely at the prefactor:<br>
- $ \nu_n $: This is the frequency of a specific nuclear vibration—the &quot;promoting mode&quot;—that helps the system reach the transition state for electron transfer. We assume one dominant mode couples to the electron transfer process. In metal complexes, this is often a symmetric &quot;breathing&quot; motion of the ligands or the metal-ligand bonds, typically in the 500–600 cm⁻¹ range (mid-infrared frequencies). Think of it as the molecule rhythmically expanding and contracting, momentarily creating the right geometry for the electron to jump.<br>
- $ \kappa $: This is the **electronic coupling factor**, which accounts for the quantum mechanical probability of the electron tunneling between the two sites. It’s dimensionless and typically less than or equal to 1. We’ll come back to how it’s calculated.<br>
<br>
So $ k_+ $ is the rate when the field assists the transfer, $ k_- $ when it opposes it. The net flow of electrons—the current—depends on the *difference* between these two rates.<br>
<br>
Let’s now compute that difference: $ k_+ - k_- $. If the electric field is small, the third term in equation (12), $ (eEa)^2/(4\lambda) $, is negligible. Then $ E_+ $ and $ E_- $ differ only by the linear term $ \pm eEa/2 $. Plugging into (13) and expanding the exponentials using a Taylor approximation (since $ eEa \ll \lambda $), we get:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
k_+ - k_- = \nu_n \kappa \left[ \exp\left(-\frac{E_+}{k_B T}\right) - \exp\left(-\frac{E_-}{k_B T}\right) \right] \approx \nu_n \kappa \cdot \frac{eEa}{k_B T} \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{4k_B T}\right) \quad (14)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Here’s the intuition: the exponential term $ \exp(-\lambda/(4k_B T)) $ is the probability of reaching the transition state (the top of the barrier), while $ eEa/(k_B T) $ measures how much the field *biases* the process in one direction. The larger the field or the distance $ a $, the greater the imbalance in rates.<br>
<br>
Now, recall that electric current $ J $ is charge per unit time per unit area. If each transfer moves charge $ e $, and there are $ n $ such charge carriers per unit volume, then the current density is roughly $ J = n e a (k_+ - k_-) $, where $ a $ converts rate per molecule to flux (like drift velocity times density). Substituting (14):<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\sigma = \frac{J}{E} = \nu_n \kappa \cdot \frac{n e^2 a^2}{k_B T} \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{4k_B T}\right) \quad (16)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
This is the **conductivity** $ \sigma $, defined as current density per unit electric field. It’s valid when the driving force (like $ \Delta G $) is small—meaning we’re in the linear response regime, close to equilibrium.<br>
<br>
Now, what about $ \kappa $? From prior work [3], it’s given by:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\kappa = 2 \left[ \frac{1 - \exp(-\nu_{el}/2\nu_n)}{2 - \exp(-\nu_{el}/2\nu_n)} \right]; \quad \nu_{el} = \frac{\Delta_{12}}{4\pi\hbar} \left( \frac{\pi^3}{\lambda k_B T} \right)^{1/2} \quad (15)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Let’s demystify this. First, $ \nu_{el} $ is called the **electronic frequency**—it’s not a real oscillation, but a characteristic timescale associated with the electron tunneling process itself. It depends on:<br>
- $ \Delta_{12} $: the electronic coupling matrix element between the two sites. Stronger coupling means faster tunneling.<br>
- $ \hbar $: the reduced Planck’s constant.<br>
- The temperature and reorganization energy in the square root term, which comes from the shape of the thermal distribution of nuclear states.<br>
<br>
Meanwhile, $ \nu_n $ is the nuclear frequency—the rate at which the molecular vibrations &quot;sample&quot; configurations that allow electron transfer.<br>
<br>
The expression for $ \kappa $ captures how well the nuclear motion synchronizes with the electronic transition. If nuclear motions are slow compared to electronic ones ($ \nu_{el} \ll \nu_n $), the system has time to adjust fully—this is the adiabatic limit. In that case, we can approximate $ \kappa \approx \nu_{el}/\nu_n $.<br>
<br>
Substituting this into (16), we get:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\sigma = \frac{J}{E} = \frac{n e^2 a^2}{k_B T} \cdot \frac{\Delta_{12}}{4\pi\hbar} \left( \frac{\pi^3}{\lambda k_B T} \right)^{1/2} \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{4k_B T}\right) \quad (17)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
This version of the conductivity is particularly insightful. Notice:<br>
- It’s proportional to $ \Delta_{12}^2 $: stronger electronic coupling leads to higher conductivity, as expected.<br>
- It’s independent of $ \nu_n $: in the adiabatic limit, the nuclear frequency doesn’t limit the rate—the bottleneck is instead the thermal activation over the barrier.<br>
- The temperature dependence is non-monotonic: at low $ T $, the exponential dominates and $ \sigma $ increases with $ T $; at high $ T $, the $ 1/T^{3/2} $ factor wins and $ \sigma $ decreases.<br>
<br>
This behavior is shown in Fig. 5: conductivity rises with temperature to a peak (around 200 K in underdoped cuprates), then falls—just like in metals. But here, it’s not free electrons; it’s localized carriers hopping via thermally activated tunneling.<br>
<br>
Now, consider the opposite limit: $ \nu_{el} \gg \nu_n $. This is the **non-adiabatic** limit—electronic coupling is weak, so tunneling is slow compared to nuclear motion. The system rarely finds itself in a configuration where transfer can occur. In this case, $ \kappa \to 1 $, and equation (16) becomes:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\sigma = \frac{J}{E} = \nu_n \frac{n e^2 a^2}{k_B T} \exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{k_B T}\right) \quad (18)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Here, $ E_a $ would be the activation energy—possibly related to $ \lambda/4 $, depending on the system.<br>
<br>
If $ E_a \to 0 $, the exponential becomes 1, and $ \sigma \propto 1/T $. This is reminiscent of the **Einstein relation** $ \sigma = n e^2 D / k_B T $, where diffusion constant $ D \propto \nu_n a^2 $. So in this limit, electron transfer behaves like classical diffusion.<br>
<br>
In crystalline electron transfer systems, we often see conductivity first increasing with temperature (activated behavior), reaching a maximum, then decreasing (metallic-like). This peak arises naturally from the competition between thermal activation and the $ 1/T $ prefactor.<br>
<br>
At very high temperatures or strong coupling, the barrier becomes negligible. This is the **delocalization limit**, described by Hush and Reimers: when the reorganization energy $ \lambda &lt; 2|\Delta_{12}| $ (or in some notations $ U &lt; 2|H_{12}| $), the electron is no longer localized on one site—it’s shared, like in a band. This is the crossover from hopping to band-like transport.<br>
<br>
Using resistivity $ \rho = 1/\sigma $, we can find the temperature $ T_{\text{min}} $ at which $ \rho $ is minimum (i.e., $ \sigma $ is maximum). Suppose $ \sigma \propto T^{-m} \exp(-E_a/k_B T) $, then:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
\rho = T^m \exp\left(\frac{E_a}{k_B T}\right) \quad (19)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
Minimizing $ \rho $ with respect to $ T $ gives:<br>
<br>
$$<br>
T_{\text{min}} = \frac{1}{m} \cdot \frac{E_a}{k_B} \quad (20)<br>
$$<br>
<br>
In the Marcus model, $ m = 3/2 $ (from the $ T^{-3/2} $ dependence in the prefactor of (17)), so $ T_{\text{min}} = \frac{2}{3} \frac{E_a}{k_B} $. This provides a way to extract activation parameters from experimental data.<br>
<br>
However, at very low temperatures, the Marcus model—which assumes classical nuclear motion and thermal activation—begins to break down. Quantum effects like tunneling through the barrier become important, and the model needs updating to include nuclear quantum effects.</div></td>
