Over the last 10 years, medical device design has vastly benefited from the integration of finite element analyses in the product development process. But what exactly is the finite element method, and why has it become such a game-changer in this field? Let&#x27;s break it down.<br>
<br>
The finite element method (FEM) is a numerical method used to solve boundary value problems. To understand what that means, imagine you want to know how a hip implant will bear weight inside a patient&#x27;s body. You can&#x27;t simply cut open a patient to measure the stresses, and building a physical prototype for every design tweak would be prohibitively expensive and slow. Instead, FEM offers a clever workaround: it breaks a complex shape—like that hip implant—into thousands or even millions of tiny, simple pieces called &quot;elements.&quot; Each element is small enough that its behavior can be described by relatively simple mathematical equations. By computing how each tiny piece reacts to forces and then stitching all those local reactions together, the method gives us a highly accurate picture of how the entire device will behave under real-world conditions.<br>
<br>
This method adopts an approach of computing reactions over a discrete number of points across the domain of interest. The &quot;domain&quot; is simply the entire region we care about—say, the implant plus the surrounding bone. The &quot;discrete points&quot; are the corners (or nodes) of those tiny elements. Instead of trying to solve one impossibly complex equation for the whole domain, FEM solves many simpler equations at these points, then assembles the results. This is the core trick of FEM: *divide and conquer*.<br>
<br>
For medical design, this typically translates towards verifying device performance in a virtual domain that is representative of its planned real-life application. In other words, we build a digital twin of the device and its environment—complete with realistic material properties, loads, and constraints—and then we run the simulation to see how the device would perform. A user may leverage results from such an analysis to interpret device performance and make educated recommendations for improvement and optimization [1–3]. For instance, if the simulation shows that a certain region of a stent experiences dangerously high stress, the designer can modify the geometry, rerun the simulation, and confirm the fix—all before cutting a single piece of metal.<br>
<br>
Moreover, predictions of initial and long-term device performance *in vivo* (that is, inside the living body) may also be derived from FEM results, often through multi-scaled and iterative analyses [4, 5]. This is a crucial point: the body is not a static environment. Bone remodels, tissues grow, and materials degrade. By running multiple simulations that account for these changes over time, engineers can predict not just whether a device will work on day one, but whether it will still be safe and effective years later. Such foresight may subsequently lead to better-prepared *in vivo* studies [6, 7], meaning that when the device finally reaches animal or human trials, the researchers already have a strong, data-driven hypothesis about what to expect—saving time, money, and potentially lives.<br>
<br>
A key acknowledgement that enables such post-processing realizations is having an experienced user working with an interdisciplinary team. This cannot be overstated. FEM software is a powerful tool, but it is not a magic box. It requires a skilled engineer to set up the problem correctly, to choose the right element types, to apply the right boundary conditions, and to interpret the results with a critical eye. And because the device will interact with a biological system, that engineer must work alongside clinicians, biologists, and material scientists. Such a team may then confidently navigate through the many advantages and disadvantages put forth by this virtual test platform, which continues to grow in popularity.<br>
<br>
---<br>
<br>
### The Advantages of FEM in Medical Device Design<br>
<br>
The advantages of FEM in the medical device arena are many. The most attractive being the speed at which the FEM can enable early device performance testing prior to costly prototyping and bench testing. Imagine the traditional product development cycle: you design a device, you build a physical prototype, you test it on the bench, you find a flaw, you redesign, you build another prototype, and so on. Each cycle takes weeks and costs thousands of dollars. With FEM, you can run dozens of design iterations in a single day, all on a computer, and only build a physical prototype once you are confident the design is solid.<br>
<br>
Correspondingly, integration of the FEM process into product development may reduce costs over the entire product development cycle. Such savings come to fruition by tentatively speeding up the process and reducing bench testing iterations. That is, bench testing may be performed via the FEM *in silico* (meaning &quot;in silicon,&quot; i.e., on a computer) instead of on the bench side or *ex vivo* (outside the living body, in a lab setup). This shift from physical to virtual testing is not just about saving money—it also allows for far more thorough exploration of the design space. You can test a hundred variations of a device in the time it would take to build and test one physical prototype.<br>
<br>
### The disadvantages of FEM for medical device design<br>
<br>
However, it would be misleading to present FEM as a perfect solution. The disadvantages of the FEM for medical device design reside mainly with the high expertise required to properly navigate the computational platform while avoiding making costly mistakes from ambitious misinterpretations. The software is complex, and it is easy to get a result that looks plausible but is actually wrong—perhaps because the mesh was too coarse, the boundary conditions were unrealistic, or the material model was oversimplified. A novice user might not recognize these errors and could make design decisions based on faulty data. This is why the &quot;experienced user&quot; mentioned earlier is so critical.<br>
<br>
Further, established product development strategies must be revised to integrate the FEM into the early design phase, which will take considerable effort for companies. Many companies have a well-oiled, traditional development process. Inserting a new computational step at the beginning requires retraining staff, changing workflows, and convincing management that the upfront investment will pay off later. This is not a trivial task. Thus, the FEM has not completely shaken the perception of being a research tool rather than a development asset just yet. In academia, FEM is used to explore fundamental questions and publish papers. In industry, it is still sometimes viewed as a &quot;nice-to-have&quot; rather than a &quot;must-have.&quot; But this perception is slowly changing as more and more companies see the tangible benefits.<br>
<br>

<br>
### Becoming a Skilled FEM User: Verification and Validation<br>
<br>
One may become a skilled and experienced FEM user through extensive knowledge of the mechanics behind the solver and awareness and implementation of verification and validation efforts. These two concepts—verification and validation—are the bedrock of trustworthy simulation. They are often confused, so let&#x27;s clarify them with a simple analogy.<br>
<br>
Imagine you are baking a cake. **Verification** is asking: &quot;Did I follow the recipe correctly? Did I measure the flour right, set the oven to the right temperature, and bake for the right time?&quot; It&#x27;s about checking that you executed the steps correctly. **Validation** is asking: &quot;Is this the right recipe for the cake I want?&quot; If you wanted a chocolate cake but you used a recipe for a sponge cake, no matter how perfectly you followed the recipe, you will not get a chocolate cake. Validation is about checking that you are solving the *right problem*.<br>
<br>
A specific and recommended guide now exists for verification and validation of numerical models in solid mechanics [8] and other fields. This guide provides a standardized framework for ensuring that your simulations are trustworthy.<br>
<br>
**Verification** is concerned with whether the relations or equations are being solved correctly. Most commercial programs undergo stringent verification procedures prior to release. The software vendor has already checked that their code correctly solves the equations it claims to solve. However, the functionality and proper convergence of the newly programmed constitutive formulations used to represent the intricacies of a physiological environment of the medical device must be performed and reported. Let&#x27;s unpack this. A &quot;constitutive formulation&quot; is a mathematical model that describes how a material behaves under load—for example, how a soft tissue stretches, or how a metal deforms. Off-the-shelf software includes standard models (like linear elasticity), but the human body is complex, and you may need to program a custom model to capture its behavior. This new model is your own code, and you must verify that it is implemented correctly. You must check that it converges to the correct answer as you refine the mesh, and you must report these checks.<br>
<br>
Furthermore, in addition to individual verification of such formulas, mechanical algorithms may give rise to new potential errors or instabilities when combined. This is a subtle but important point. Even if each individual component of your simulation is correct, when you combine them—say, a custom tissue model with a contact algorithm and a large-deformation solver—the interaction can introduce new errors. Such compounded relations must also be verified and accounted for. This is like checking that each instrument in an orchestra is in tune, but also ensuring that the orchestra as a whole plays harmoniously together.<br>
<br>
Once the FEM appears to be verified, additional sensitivity or probabilistic analyses should be conducted to further weight the impact of your assumptions on the results [9]. This is a crucial step that is often skipped. Your simulation is built on assumptions: the material properties you chose, the boundary conditions you applied, the mesh density you used. But how sensitive is your result to these assumptions? If you change the material stiffness by 10%, does your predicted stress change by 1% or by 50%? A sensitivity analysis systematically varies these inputs to see how much the outputs fluctuate.<br>
<br>
If too much fluctuation in the results is initiated from the sensitivity study, the variable&#x27;s magnitude must be selected carefully and results viewed and interpreted cautiously. In other words, if your result is highly sensitive to a particular assumption, then that assumption is critical. You need to be very sure about its value, and you should be cautious about drawing strong conclusions from your simulation. If little fluctuation ensues, then the formulation under consideration is robust. This is a great outcome: it means your model is stable and your conclusions are likely reliable.<br>
<br>
In contrast to verification, **validation** is concerned with whether the correct relations or equations are being used. Thus, in other words, are you using the right mathematics that agree with the mechanics of the FEM solver? Going back to our cake analogy, validation asks: &quot;Is this the right recipe for the cake I want?&quot; In the context of FEM, it asks: &quot;Is my mathematical model actually capturing the real physical behavior of the device and its environment?&quot; For example, if you are simulating a heart valve, are you using the correct equations for blood flow and tissue deformation? If you use a model that assumes the tissue is rigid when it is actually flexible, your results will be meaningless, no matter how perfectly you solve the equations.<br>
<br>
The most convincing form of validation is done experimentally by way of an experiment that is representative of the FEM simulated boundary value problem. This means you set up a physical experiment that mimics the conditions of your simulation as closely as possible. You apply the same loads, use the same materials, and measure the same outputs. This form of direct validation will either support or discredit what was reported utilizing the FEM platform. If the experimental results match your simulation results, you have strong evidence that your model is correct. If they do not match, you must go back and figure out why—perhaps your boundary conditions were wrong, your material model was inaccurate, or your mesh was too coarse.<br>

<br>
Over the last 10 years, medical device design has vastly benefited from the integration of finite element analyses in the product development process. But what exactly is the *finite element method* (FEM), and why has it become such a game-changer in this field? Let’s break it down step by step.<br>
<br>
At its core, the finite element method (FEM) is a powerful numerical technique used to solve complex engineering and physical problems—specifically, boundary value problems. Think of a boundary value problem as a real-world scenario where we want to understand how something behaves (like stress in a bone or blood flow in an artery), but only if we know the conditions at its edges or boundaries (like how much force is applied or what the pressure is at the ends). These problems are governed by partial differential equations—mathematical expressions that describe how physical quantities change in space and time. Solving them exactly is often impossible for real-life geometries, which is where FEM comes in.<br>
<br>
So how does FEM work? Instead of trying to solve the equations for the entire complex structure all at once, FEM breaks the structure down into thousands—or even millions—of tiny, manageable pieces called *finite elements*. These elements are connected at specific points called *nodes*. The method then computes how each small piece responds to forces, pressures, or other loads, and how those responses interact across the entire system. It&#x27;s like solving a giant puzzle by first understanding each tiny piece and then seeing how they fit together to form the full picture.<br>
<br>
For medical device design, this approach is transformative. Engineers can build a digital twin—a virtual 3D model—of a medical device (like a stent, hip implant, or spinal screw) and simulate how it will behave inside the human body. This virtual domain is carefully designed to mimic real-life biological conditions, such as the stiffness of bone, the pulsing of blood, or the movement of joints. By running FEM simulations, designers can predict how the device will perform *before* ever building a physical prototype.<br>
<br>
This means a user can leverage FEM results not just to see *if* a device works, but *how well* it works—and where it might fail. These insights allow engineers to interpret device performance with high precision and make educated, data-driven recommendations for improvement and optimization [1–3]. For example, if a simulated hip implant shows high stress concentrations in a certain region, the design can be modified to redistribute that stress, potentially preventing fractures in real patients.<br>
<br>
But FEM’s power doesn’t stop at initial performance. One of its most exciting applications is predicting *long-term* behavior in the body—what we call *in vivo* performance. The human body is dynamic: tissues remodel, loads change over time, and implants interact with living systems in complex ways. To capture this, engineers often use *multi-scaled and iterative analyses* [4, 5]. This means running simulations at different levels of detail (from the whole implant down to the microscopic structure of bone) and repeating the process with updated conditions—like how a bone might adapt to an implant over months or years. These predictions help researchers anticipate complications and refine designs to improve safety and longevity.<br>
<br>
As a result, when it comes time to conduct actual animal or human studies (*in vivo* testing), the team is much better prepared. They’re not guessing in the dark; they’re testing informed hypotheses. This can lead to more efficient, ethical, and successful clinical trials [6, 7].<br>
<br>
However, none of this is possible without a crucial ingredient: *an experienced user working within an interdisciplinary team*. FEM is not a push-button tool. It requires deep understanding of mechanics, material behavior, computational methods, and—just as importantly—biological systems. A mechanical engineer might build a perfect simulation, but without input from a surgeon or biologist, the model might miss critical real-world factors like tissue healing or patient variability. So, the team must include experts from different fields who can collectively interpret the results and ensure the simulation truly reflects clinical reality.<br>
<br>
This brings us to a key point: FEM is more than just software. It’s a *virtual test platform*—a digital laboratory where ideas can be tested, refined, and validated with speed and precision. And as this platform continues to grow in popularity, teams that master it gain a significant advantage in innovation and efficiency.<br>
<br>
Now, let’s talk about *why* FEM has become so attractive in medical device development. The advantages are numerous, but perhaps the most compelling is *speed*. Traditionally, designing a new medical device involved building multiple physical prototypes, testing them in the lab (bench testing), and iterating based on results. This process is not only slow but also expensive—each prototype can cost thousands of dollars, and testing setups require specialized equipment and time.<br>
<br>
FEM changes this equation entirely. With a well-constructed model, engineers can simulate device performance in *silico*—on the computer—within hours or days, not weeks or months. This allows for rapid exploration of design alternatives: “What if we change the thickness here?” “What if the material is slightly softer?” These “what-if” scenarios can be tested virtually, dramatically accelerating the early design phase.<br>
<br>
As a result, the integration of FEM into product development can significantly *reduce costs* over the entire development cycle. How? By reducing the number of physical prototypes needed and minimizing the number of bench testing iterations. Instead of building five versions of a device to find the best one, you might build only one or two—because the rest were already tested and optimized in simulation.<br>
<br>
In essence, FEM allows you to do bench testing *without* the bench—*in silico* instead of *ex vivo* (outside the living body) or on physical test stands. This doesn’t mean physical testing becomes obsolete—far from it. Real-world validation is still essential. But FEM ensures that when you do test physically, you’re testing a much more refined, well-understood design.<br>
<br>
Yet, for all its benefits, FEM is not without challenges. The biggest disadvantage? The *high level of expertise* required to use it correctly. FEM software is powerful, but it’s also unforgiving. If you input incorrect material properties, oversimplify a boundary condition, or mesh the geometry too coarsely, the simulation might run perfectly—but give completely wrong results. And because the output often looks so polished (with colorful stress maps and smooth animations), it’s easy to be misled by “beautiful garbage.”<br>
<br>
This risk is especially high in medical applications, where biological systems are inherently complex and variable. An inexperienced user might make *ambitious misinterpretations*—drawing strong conclusions from a model that doesn’t accurately represent reality. That’s why proper training and rigorous methodology are non-negotiable.<br>
<br>
Moreover, adopting FEM isn’t just about learning new software—it requires a shift in *product development strategy*. Companies must integrate FEM into the *early design phase*, not just use it as a final check. This means investing in training, computational resources, and interdisciplinary collaboration from day one. For many organizations, especially smaller ones, this transition demands considerable effort and cultural change.<br>
<br>
Because of these barriers, FEM has not yet fully shaken off the perception of being a *research tool*—something academics use in labs—rather than a core *development asset* used routinely in industry. But as more success stories emerge and best practices become standardized, that perception is slowly shifting.<br>
<br>
So, how does one become a skilled and experienced FEM user? It starts with a deep understanding of the *mechanics behind the solver*—the mathematical engine that crunches the numbers. You don’t need to write the code yourself, but you *do* need to understand how it works: what assumptions it makes, how it handles nonlinearities, and how it converges to a solution.<br>
<br>
Equally important is a strong commitment to *verification and validation*—two concepts that are often confused but are fundamentally different. Let’s clarify them.<br>
<br>
*Verification* asks: **&quot;Are we solving the equations correctly?&quot;** In other words, is the software accurately computing the mathematical solution to the model you’ve built? Most commercial FEM software (like ANSYS, Abaqus, or COMSOL) undergoes rigorous verification by the developers before release. But that doesn’t mean your *specific model* is correct. You still need to verify that your implementation—especially any custom material models or user-defined subroutines—is working as intended.<br>
<br>
For example, if you’re simulating a soft biological tissue, you might use a special *constitutive formulation*—a mathematical model that describes how the tissue deforms under load. These formulations can be highly complex, capturing nonlinear elasticity, viscoelasticity, or anisotropy (different behavior in different directions). Before trusting your results, you must verify that this formulation is coded correctly and that the solver converges properly under various conditions.<br>
<br>
But here’s a subtle but critical point: even if each individual component is verified, *combining them can introduce new errors or instabilities*. For instance, a material model might work fine on its own, and a contact algorithm might work fine on its own—but when used together in a simulation of a joint implant, they might interact in unexpected ways, causing the solution to diverge or oscillate. These *compounded relations* must also be verified and carefully documented.<br>
<br>
Once verification is complete, the next step is *sensitivity analysis* or *probabilistic analysis*. These techniques help you understand how sensitive your results are to the assumptions you’ve made—like the exact value of a material’s stiffness or the magnitude of an applied load. You intentionally vary these inputs within a reasonable range and observe how much the output (e.g., stress or displacement) changes.<br>
<br>
If the results fluctuate wildly with small changes in input, that’s a red flag. It means your conclusions are highly dependent on assumptions that may not be precisely known—so you must select those variable values very carefully, and interpret the results with caution. On the other hand, if the results remain stable despite variations, your model is *robust*, and your conclusions are more trustworthy.<br>
<br>
Now, let’s turn to *validation*. This asks a different question: **&quot;Are we solving the right equations?&quot;** In other words, does your model accurately represent the real-world physics of the problem? Verification ensures the math is correct; validation ensures the *physics* is correct.<br>
<br>
The gold standard for validation is *experimental testing*. You design a physical experiment that closely matches the conditions of your FEM simulation—a real-world version of the same boundary value problem. For example, if you simulated a spinal implant under compression, you’d test an actual implant in a mechanical testing machine under the same loading conditions.<br>
<br>
If the experimental results (e.g., how much the implant deforms) match the simulation results, your model is validated—or at least supported—for that specific scenario. If they don’t match, you need to go back and refine your assumptions: maybe the material model is wrong, or the boundary conditions don’t reflect reality.<br>
