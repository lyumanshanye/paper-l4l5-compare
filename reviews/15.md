<td valign="top"><div>the achiral structure of the bar-type nanoslits cannot distinguish the helicity of light.  <br>
This means that whether the incoming light is right-handed (clockwise rotation of the electric field) or left-handed (counterclockwise), the bar-shaped nanostructures respond in exactly the same way. They lack any inherent &quot;handedness&quot; or chirality in their design—like a plain straight road that doesn’t care which direction you turn onto it. As a result, they cannot tell the difference between the two circular polarizations, denoted as $|\sigma_+\rangle$ (right-handed) and $|\sigma_-\rangle$ (left-handed). This symmetry limitation prevents them from exhibiting polarization-dependent responses, such as selectively enhancing or suppressing one helicity over the other.<br>
<br>
For the /C22Z-type metasurfaces, Fig. 5(a1,b1,c1,d1) shows SEM images of $q = 1, -1, 2,$ and $-2$, respectively. Let’s unpack what this means. The parameter $q$ refers to the topological charge of the metasurface, which essentially dictates how the orientation of the individual building blocks—called metamolecules—rotates as you move around the ring. A positive $q$ means the rotation follows a counterclockwise twist; a negative $q$, a clockwise twist. The magnitude tells us how many full rotations occur over one circumference. So for $q = 1$, the metamolecules complete one full turn around the ring; for $q = 2$, two turns, and so on.<br>
<br>
Now, looking at these SEM images, we see that even though the metamolecules have been replaced—from simple bars to more complex /C22Z-shaped elements—the overall symmetry of the metasurface remains unchanged. In other words, the global geometric pattern still respects the same rotational symmetries as before. Specifically:<br>
- The circular symmetry corresponds to $q = 1$,<br>
- Square symmetry to $q = -1$,<br>
- Linear (or bilateral) symmetry to $q = 2$,<br>
- Hexagonal symmetry to $q = -2$.<br>
<br>
This preservation of symmetry is crucial because it allows us to isolate the effect of the metamolecule’s shape—its local chirality—without being confounded by changes in the global geometry.<br>
<br>
Under $|\sigma_+\rangle$ illumination (right-handed circularly polarized light), we observe specific near-field interference patterns via scanning near-field optical microscopy (SNOM), shown in Fig. 5(a2,b2,c2,d2), along with corresponding finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations in Fig. 5(a3,b3,c3,d3). Remarkably, the symmetry of the resulting surface plasmon polariton (SPP) field patterns still matches the geometric symmetry of each metasurface. This tells us that the macroscopic arrangement continues to guide the overall symmetry of the excited electromagnetic field.<br>
<br>
However, the fine details—the intricate interference fringes—look very different from those seen with the bar-type metasurfaces. Why? Because now, each /C22Z-shaped metamolecule is itself chiral: it has a built-in handedness. In fact, these /C22Z units are planar chiral and specifically left-handed. Think of them like tiny spiral staircases etched flat into the surface, all turning in the same direction—say, like a left-handed screw when viewed from above.<br>
<br>
When right-handed circularly polarized light ($|\sigma_+\rangle$) hits these left-handed chiral structures, something fascinating happens. The incident light tries to excite surface plasmons that carry a certain orbital angular momentum—a kind of &quot;twist&quot; in the wavefront. But because the metamolecule is left-handed, it naturally wants to generate a vortex-like SPP field that circulates in the opposite direction (counterclockwise, if we define the coordinate system appropriately).<br>
<br>
This leads to a clash: the geometric phase induced by the metasurface structure and the spin-orbit interaction from the circular polarization produce fields with opposing circulation. When these two effects are out of sync—when the handedness of the light doesn’t match the handedness of the structure—they interfere destructively in the near field.<br>
<br>
Nowhere is this more evident than in Fig. 5(a2): for the $q = 1$ metasurface under $|\sigma_+\rangle$ illumination, almost no SNOM signal is detected. That means there&#x27;s very little light-matter interaction happening at the surface—very few plasmons are being excited. The simulation in Fig. 5(a3) confirms this: the calculated electric field intensity is extremely weak, roughly an order of magnitude lower than for the other cases. It’s as if the structure and the light are “speaking different languages”—they can’t communicate effectively.<br>
<br>
Why does this destructive interference happen so strongly for $q = 1$ and not as completely for others? Because the $q = 1$ structure has circular symmetry, which supports the formation of a clean, coherent vortex field. When that ideal vortex is excited with the wrong helicity, the mismatch is total, leading to near-perfect cancellation. But for non-circularly symmetric structures—like the square ($q = -1$), linear ($q = 2$), or hexagonal ($q = -2$)—the symmetry breaks the perfection of the vortex. You can’t form a smooth, continuous whirlpool on a square-shaped track. So while some destructive interference still occurs, it’s incomplete. That’s why in Fig. 5(b3,c3,d3), we see residual fringe patterns lingering alongside the main symmetric features—“relics” of partial interference, where some plasmonic activity survives despite the mismatch.<br>
<br>
But now, flip the polarization: use left-handed circularly polarized light, $|\sigma_-\rangle$, on the $q = 1$ metasurface. Suddenly, everything clicks. The incident light has the same handedness as the chiral metamolecules. Now, instead of fighting each other, the geometric phase and the spin-induced field reinforce one another. Constructive interference takes over. This is clearly seen in Fig. 5(a4,a5), where strong, well-defined interference fringes appear in both measurement and simulation. The structure and the light are now in harmony, efficiently coupling energy into the SPPs.<br>
<br>
For the other symmetric metasurfaces [Fig. 5(b,c,d)], the near-field SPP patterns under $|\sigma_+\rangle$ illumination resemble those of the bar-type counterparts—but with completely different fringe details. This isn’t just random noise; it reflects the new physics introduced by the chiral metamolecules. The interference landscape is reshaped by the local chirality, making the response highly sensitive to the input polarization.<br>
<br>
The big takeaway? Unlike the achiral bar-type designs, these chiral /C22Z metasurfaces *can* distinguish between left- and right-handed light. They respond strongly to one helicity and weakly (or not at all) to the other, depending on their structural handedness. This selective response based on spin—on the circular polarization state—is a hallmark of the **optical spin Hall effect**. It’s a direct manifestation of spin-orbit coupling in nanophotonics, where the &quot;spin&quot; of light (its polarization) influences the &quot;orbit&quot; (its spatial trajectory or mode structure) via structured interfaces.<br>
<br>
It is noted that the results of the Z-type metasurface are completely opposite to those of the /C22Z-type one and are not shown here.  <br>
This implies that the Z-type structures are likely right-handed chiral versions of the same design. So they would couple strongly to $|\sigma_+\rangle$ light and weakly to $|\sigma_-\rangle$, the reverse of /C22Z. This symmetry complementarity further underscores the role of structural chirality in controlling light at the nanoscale.<br>
