formation by the pericytes (Winkler et al., 2011). Let’s unpack what this means. Pericytes are support cells embedded in the basement membrane of blood vessels, and they play a critical role in shaping the blood-brain barrier (BBB) as it develops. One of their key contributions is helping to form tight junctions between brain endothelial cells (BECs)—the specialized cells that line the brain’s capillaries and act as the primary gatekeepers of the BBB. These tight junctions are like molecular zippers that seal the gaps between adjacent cells, preventing unwanted substances from leaking into the brain.<br>
<br>
Now, how do pericytes influence this process? They don’t just physically wrap around vessels—they send chemical signals. Two important signaling pathways involved are **Notch** and **sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P)**. These pathways regulate the expression of **cadherin-2**, a protein that helps endothelial cells stick to each other. Think of cadherin-2 as the “Velcro” that holds neighboring cells together at adherens junctions, which form the foundation for more complex tight junctions. When Notch or S1P signaling is activated, they help stabilize these early cell-cell connections, setting the stage for a fully sealed barrier (Winkler et al., 2011; Obermeier et al., 2013).<br>
<br>
But pericytes do more than just help with structural adhesion. When **Tgf-β signaling** is activated in pericytes, they respond by producing a molecule called **Angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1)**. Ang-1 acts on the endothelial cells, boosting the formation of tight junctions—think of it as adding extra layers to the molecular zipper. This makes the barrier less leaky, reducing BBB permeability. At the same time, Ang-1 reduces the expression of **leukocyte adhesion molecules (LAMs)**—proteins that immune cells use to latch onto blood vessel walls. By suppressing LAMs, Ang-1 helps maintain immune privilege in the brain, limiting unnecessary inflammation. This dual action—tightening the barrier and quieting immune recruitment—is a key way pericytes help establish a functional BBB.<br>
<br>
Meanwhile, other signaling systems are also hard at work. The **Pdgf-β** (platelet-derived growth factor beta) pathway is crucial for recruiting pericytes to the developing blood vessels in the first place. Without Pdgf-β signaling, pericytes don’t migrate properly to the endothelium, leading to a weak and leaky BBB. Once pericytes are in place, another major player steps in: the **Wnt/β-catenin** signaling pathway. This pathway is like the master regulator of BBB identity. It drives the differentiation of brain endothelial cells by turning on genes that code for tight junction proteins and specialized transporters—molecular “gates” that allow essential nutrients like glucose to enter the brain while keeping toxins out.<br>
<br>
For example, Wnt signaling ensures high expression of **Glut-1**, a glucose transporter vital for brain energy supply. It also promotes the expression of other transporters and junctional proteins, giving the BBB its unique selective permeability (Liebner et al., 2008; Daneman et al., 2009; Tam et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2012; Zhou and Nathans, 2014; Andreone et al., 2015). In essence, Wnt/β-catenin signaling doesn’t just tweak the BBB—it defines it.<br>
<br>
Within this system, a special receptor called **Gpr124** has emerged as a critical co-activator of Wnt signaling—but only in the central nervous system (CNS). Gpr124 is found on endothelial cells and works hand-in-hand with other Wnt pathway components to ensure that Wnt signals are strong and specific enough to mature the BBB properly. Without Gpr124, Wnt signaling in brain vessels is impaired, leading to defective barrier formation (Kuhnert et al., 2010; Cullen et al., 2011; Zhou and Nathans, 2014).<br>
<br>
Now, let’s bring in the next key player: **astrocytes**. These star-shaped glial cells are recruited to blood vessels after pericytes, and pericytes actually help guide them into position (Armulik et al., 2010). Once astrocytes arrive and extend their endfeet to wrap around the vessels, they become active participants in BBB regulation.<br>
<br>
One of their most important contributions is the release of **Sonic Hedgehog (Shh)**, a powerful signaling molecule. When Shh binds to receptors on endothelial cells, it triggers a cascade that increases the expression of two key tight junction proteins: **occludin** and **claudin-5**. Claudin-5, in particular, is essential for sealing the paracellular pathway—the space between cells. At the same time, Shh signaling suppresses the production of chemokines and cell adhesion molecules, which would otherwise attract immune cells. This means astrocytes not only tighten the barrier but also help maintain the brain’s immune-privileged status, reducing the risk of neuroinflammation (Alvarez et al., 2011; Obermeier et al., 2013; Siegenthaler et al., 2013).<br>
<br>
But that’s not all astrocytes do. Recent research has revealed another vital role: they produce **retinoic acid**, a derivative of vitamin A. Retinoic acid acts on endothelial cells to support the proper development of BBB-specific vessels. It helps ensure that the vascular network in the brain acquires the right structural and functional characteristics, including appropriate tight junction formation and transporter expression (Halilagic et al., 2007; Mizee et al., 2013).<br>
<br>
So, how does the BBB reach full maturity and stay functional over time? Maturation isn’t a one-time event—it’s an ongoing process. It depends on the sustained expression and proper localization of tight junction proteins across the entire vascular network. The **Wnt signaling pathway**, particularly through communication between astrocytes and endothelial cells, continues to regulate this process even after initial development.<br>
<br>
Moreover, the integrity of the BBB relies on the close physical and biochemical interactions among multiple cell types: endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, and potentially neurons and microglia. Together, they form a dynamic and stabilized unit known as the **neurovascular unit (NVU)**. This multicellular teamwork ensures that the BBB remains responsive to neural activity, metabolic demands, and potential threats (reviewed in Obermeier et al., 2013; reviewed in Engelhardt and Liebner, 2014; reviewed in Zhao et al., 2015).<br>
<br>
When does this sophisticated system become operational? In rodents, the BBB is already formed and fully functional by late gestation. Specifically, in rat embryos, it becomes functional as early as embryonic day 16 (E16) (Saunders et al., 2012). In humans, this occurs during the third trimester of pregnancy (Bauer et al., 1995; Daneman et al., 2010a; Ben-Zvi et al., 2014). However, the exact timing can vary between species, and the precise window of BBB maturation is still not fully defined (Saunders et al., 2013; Hagan and Ben-Zvi, 2015).<br>
<br>
Now, an important question arises: what exactly makes the BBB *fully* functional? For a long time, scientists thought that the appearance of tight junctions was the key milestone. And indeed, studies show that tight junctions begin forming early in development and are functional from the start (Dziegielewska et al., 1979; Bondjers et al., 2006; Tam et al., 2012; Ben-Zvi et al., 2014). But more recent evidence suggests that tight junctions alone aren’t enough.<br>
<br>
A second critical factor is the regulation of **transcytosis**—the process by which substances are transported across the cell in vesicles. In most capillaries throughout the body, transcytosis is common, allowing molecules to cross via “cellular shuttles.” But in a mature BBB, this pathway is significantly reduced. Only when transcytosis is partially downregulated does the BBB achieve its full restrictive character, minimizing non-specific transport and enhancing selectivity (Hagan and Ben-Zvi, 2015).<br>
<br>
As for transporters, we know less about their functional activity at the embryonic stage, though we do see that key ones like **Glut-1** are expressed very early (Boado and Pardridge, 1990; Farrell and Pardridge, 1991; Bauer et al., 1995; Hagan and Ben-Zvi, 2015). This makes sense—developing brains need a steady supply of glucose from the start. But the full functional maturation of the transporter system likely coincides with overall BBB stabilization.<br>
