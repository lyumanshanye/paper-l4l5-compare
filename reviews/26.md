### 2.2 The Presence of O₂: Oxygen as the Polymerization Spoiler<br>
<br>
Now that we have seen how a radical polymerization proceeds under ideal conditions, we must confront a very real and pervasive problem: oxygen. The air we breathe is roughly 21% molecular oxygen (O₂), and this seemingly benign gas is a formidable enemy to radical polymerization.<br>
<br>
When a photoinitiator absorbs UV light and breaks apart to form free radicals, these highly reactive species are immediately attacked by any oxygen molecules present. The oxygen acts like a trap, quickly reacting with the radical to form a new species called a *peroxyl radical*. <br>
<br>
Let&#x27;s pause and think about this new molecule. The original radical was hungry to react with the acrylate double bond to start a polymer chain. The peroxyl radical, however, is a much more stable and &quot;lazy&quot; species. It is not reactive towards the acrylate double bonds. This means it cannot initiate a new polymer chain, nor can it participate in the growth of an existing one. In essence, every radical that reacts with oxygen is a radical that is lost for the polymerization reaction.<br>
<br>
This has a direct and costly consequence. To get the desired result—a hard, tack-free coating with good mechanical properties—you need a certain number of polymer chains to form. If oxygen is &quot;stealing&quot; your radicals, you must compensate. This means you need to add *more* photoinitiator to the formulation and expose the sample to *more* UV energy. This extra energy and initiator are not used to build the polymer network; they are simply consumed to neutralize the oxygen. This includes both the oxygen that was initially dissolved in the resin and the atmospheric oxygen that continuously diffuses into the sample during the entire UV exposure.<br>
<br>
Overcoming this unwanted reaction has turned into a major challenge. Different methods have been considered. A clear, illustrative example is reported in Figure 4, in which the effect of air and inert atmosphere (CO₂ and N₂) was investigated [Studer et al., 2003]. In the presence of air, the polymerization starts after an induction period of 0.2 s, at a speed four times lower than that in an inert atmosphere (Fig. 4). After 0.35 s, once 15% of the acrylate double bonds have polymerized, the reaction begins already to slow down because of the continuous diffusion of air into the sample. To prevent the diffusion of oxygen into the sample, a UV-transparent polypropylene (PP) film was placed on top of the liquid coating (laminated sample). The induction period is then slightly shorter than in air (oxygen is still dissolved in the film) and the polymerization proceeds three times faster than in air to reach an 85% conversion by the end of the UV-irradiation (35 s).<br>

### 2.3 The Photoinitiator: A Matter of Light Competition<br>
<br>
We have established that the photoinitiator is the engine of the reaction, but how do we measure its efficiency? The efficiency of the radical photo-initiator can be described by two quantum yields: The quantum yields of initiation, which represents the number of starting polymer chains per photons absorbed, and the quantum yields of polymerization, which is the number of monomer units polymerized per photons absorbed. <br>
<br>
Think of these as two different &quot;fuel efficiency&quot; ratings. The first tells you how many engines (polymer chains) you can start with a single unit of fuel (a photon). The second tells you how many miles (monomer units added) you can travel per unit of fuel. A highly efficient photoinitiator will have high values for both.<br>
<br>
Therefore, it is clear that if the monomers are absorbing in the same UV range as the photoinitiator, the competition will limit the photons absorbed by the latter. This can lead to a decrease of quantum yields and therefore to a lower degree of conversion as well as polymerization rate. In such cases, special care needs to be taken when selecting the proper photoinitiator. This issue becomes particularly important when dealing with formulations containing fillers which absorb light in the same spectral range as the photoinitiator.<br>
<br>

It is evident that there is a competitive absorption between the photo-initiator and the ceramic powder in the UV region between 200 and 400 nm. That leads to a linear decrease of acrylic double bond during UV irradiation by increasing the filler content in the photocurable formulation [Lombardi et al., 2011].<br>

## 2.2 Presence of O₂  <br>
<br>
Let’s step into the lab for a moment and imagine we’re shining UV light on a liquid resin to turn it into a solid coating—like curing a nail polish or a protective layer on a smartphone screen. The process starts when a *photoinitiator* molecule absorbs UV light and breaks apart into *free radicals*, which are highly reactive fragments that kickstart polymerization by attacking double bonds in acrylate monomers.  <br>
<br>
But here’s a hidden enemy: oxygen (O₂). Even though we don’t see it, O₂ is floating around in the air and dissolved in the resin. When those freshly formed free radicals meet O₂, they don’t get to start polymerization. Instead, they’re instantly intercepted and converted into *peroxyl radicals*.  <br>
<br>
Now, why does that matter?  <br>
<br>
Peroxyl radicals are like overcautious workers—they’re too stable and unreactive to attack acrylate double bonds. They just wander off without starting any chains. So, every radical that gets scavenged by oxygen is a wasted opportunity to build polymer chains. This means the polymerization reaction can’t begin in earnest until all the available oxygen is used up.  <br>
<br>
This creates two big problems:  <br>
1. **Oxygen already dissolved** in the resin must be consumed before polymerization can start.  <br>
2. **More oxygen keeps diffusing in** from the air above the sample during UV exposure, especially at the surface.  <br>
<br>
So, we’re fighting a constant battle. To win it, we need extra photoinitiator—and more UV energy—to generate enough radicals not just to start polymerization, but first to *neutralize* all the oxygen. Only then can the real curing begin.  <br>
<br>
This delay is called the **induction period**, and it directly impacts how fast and how completely the material cures. If we don’t compensate for oxygen, we end up with soft, sticky surfaces (called &quot;tackiness&quot;) and weak mechanical properties—definitely not what we want in a durable coating.  <br>
<br>
Overcoming this unwanted reaction has turned into a major challenge. Different methods  <br>
have been considered: A clear, illustrative example is reported in Figure 4 in which the effect  <br>
of air and inert atmosphere (CO₂ and N₂) was investigated [Studer et al., 2003].  <br>
<br>
Let’s look at what happens in practice. In **air**, the polymerization doesn’t start right away. There’s a **0.2-second delay**—the induction period—while radicals are busy neutralizing oxygen. When the reaction finally kicks in, it proceeds at only **one-fourth the speed** compared to an oxygen-free environment.  <br>
<br>
Even worse: after just **0.35 seconds**, when only **15%** of the acrylate double bonds have reacted, the polymerization begins to slow down. Why? Because fresh oxygen is continuously diffusing into the sample from the air above, creating a never-ending supply of radical scavengers.  <br>
<br>
Now, here’s a clever fix: what if we put a barrier on top?  <br>
<br>
Researchers placed a thin, **UV-transparent polypropylene (PP) film** over the liquid resin—like sealing it with plastic wrap. This film acts as an **oxygen barrier**, blocking atmospheric oxygen from entering. This setup is called a “laminated sample.”  <br>
<br>
The result? The induction period shortens slightly (some oxygen is still trapped in the resin itself), but now the polymerization proceeds **three times faster** than in air. And by the end of 35 seconds of UV exposure, the conversion reaches **85%**—a dramatic improvement.  <br>
<br>
This shows how critical oxygen management is in real-world applications. Whether you’re making dental fillings, optical fibers, or industrial coatings, controlling the atmosphere or using physical barriers can make the difference between success and failure.  <br>

## 2.3 Photoinitiator  <br>
<br>
Now let’s zoom in on the star of the show: the **photoinitiator**. Its job is to absorb light and generate radicals efficiently. But how do we measure its performance?  <br>
<br>
We use two key metrics called **quantum yields**—these tell us how good the photoinitiator is at converting light into useful chemical reactions.  <br>
<br>
- **Quantum yield of initiation (Φᵢ):** This is the number of *polymer chains started* per photon absorbed. Think of it as “how many fires can one spark light?”  <br>
- **Quantum yield of polymerization (Φₚ):** This is the number of *monomer units* that get added into polymer chains per photon absorbed. It reflects the overall efficiency of turning light into polymer.  <br>
<br>
Ideally, every photon absorbed would lead to a long chain of monomers being linked together. But reality gets in the way.  <br>
<br>
Here’s a critical issue: **competition for light**.  <br>
<br>
If the *monomers themselves* absorb UV light in the same wavelength range as the photoinitiator, they’re essentially stealing photons. The photoinitiator can’t generate radicals if it doesn’t get enough light.  <br>
<br>
So even if we shine plenty of UV on the sample, much of it might be absorbed by the wrong molecules—monomers or other additives—leaving the photoinitiator starved. This reduces both quantum yields, leading to **slower polymerization** and a **lower final conversion**.  <br>
<br>
This isn’t just theoretical. It becomes a real headache when formulating resins with **fillers**—solid particles added to improve mechanical strength, thermal stability, or other properties. Some fillers, like certain ceramics, also absorb UV light.  <br>
<br>
In Figure 5 the UV-Vis spectra of a radical photoinitiator and the BaTiO₃ (BT) filler that  <br>
was used in an acrylic formulation are presented. It is evident that there is a competitive  <br>
absorption between the photo-initiator and the ceramic powder in the UV region between  <br>
200 and 400 nm. That leads to a linear decrease of acrylic double bond during UV  <br>
irradiation by increasing the filler content in the photocurable formulation [Lombardi et al., 2011].  <br>
<br>
Let’s unpack that.  <br>
<br>
Fig. 5. UV-VIS spectra of the 2-hydroxy-2-methyl-1-phenyl-propan-1-one (DAROCUR®  <br>
1173, Ciba®) photo-initiator and the BaTiO₃ (BT) filler. [Lombardi et al., 2011]  <br>
<br>
You can see that both the photoinitiator (DAROCUR 1173) and the BaTiO₃ filler absorb strongly in the same UV range. As more filler is added, less light reaches the photoinitiator. It’s like trying to sunbathe under an increasingly shady tree—the more leaves (filler), the less sunlight (UV) hits your skin (photoinitiator).  <br>
<br>
The result? A **linear drop** in the rate of double bond consumption. More filler → less initiation → slower cure.  <br>
<br>
So, when designing a formulation, you can’t just pick any photoinitiator. You have to **match its absorption profile** to the transmission window of the rest of the system. Otherwise, you’re wasting energy and money.  <br>
<br>
This is why formulation science is both art and engineering: balancing reactivity, transparency, cost, and performance.  <br>
<br>
## 2.4 Monomers  <br>
<br>
Now that we understand how radicals are generated and how oxygen and light absorption affect the process, let’s shift focus to the building blocks themselves: the **monomers**.  <br>
<br>
One of the most powerful tools we have is **RT-FTIR spectroscopy** (Real-Time Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy). It lets us watch the reaction as it happens, second by second.  <br>
<br>
Specifically, we can track the disappearance of the **acrylate C=C double bond peak** around 810 cm⁻¹. As polymerization proceeds, this peak shrinks. By measuring how fast it decreases, we can calculate the **rate of polymerization (Rₚ)** at any point in time.  <br>
<br>
In fact, if we plot Rₚ against the **conversion** (i.e., how much of the double bonds have reacted), we get a kinetic profile that reveals everything: induction periods, maximum speed, and how the reaction slows down as viscosity increases or oxygen interferes.  <br>
<br>
But we can go deeper.  <br>
<br>
The **overall polymerization quantum yield (Φₚ)**—remember, the number of monomers polymerized per photon absorbed—can be calculated using a simple but powerful relationship:  <br>
<br>
\[<br>
\phi_p = \frac{R_p}{I_a}<br>
\]  <br>
<br>
where:  <br>
- \( R_p \) = rate of polymerization (in mol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹)  <br>
- \( I_a \) = intensity of *absorbed* light (in einstein·L⁻¹·s⁻¹)  <br>
<br>
This equation connects the macroscopic world (how fast the material is curing) to the microscopic world (how efficiently photons are being used).  <br>
<br>
A high Φₚ means we’re getting a lot of polymer per photon—ideal for energy-efficient curing. A low Φₚ suggests losses, perhaps due to oxygen, poor initiator choice, or competitive absorption.  <br>
